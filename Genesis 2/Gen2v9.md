# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:9

## Genesis 2:9

> **“And out of the ground the LORD God made to spring up every tree that is pleasant to the sight and good for food. The tree of life was in the midst of the garden, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וַיַּצְמַח יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים מִן־הָאֲדָמָה כָּל־עֵץ נֶחְמָד לְמַרְאֶה וְטוֹב לְמַאֲכָל וְעֵץ הַחַיִּים בְּתוֹךְ הַגָּן וְעֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע**

## Transliteration

*Wayyaṣmaḥ YHWH ʾĕlōhîm min-hāʾăḏāmāh kol-ʿēṣ neḥmād ləmarʾeh wəṭôḇ ləmaʾăḵāl wəʿēṣ haḥayyîm bəṯôḵ ha-gan wəʿēṣ haddaʿaṯ ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the LORD God caused to grow from the ground every tree desirable in appearance and good for food, and the tree of life was in the midst of the garden, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.”**

---

## 1. “And out of the ground the LORD God made to spring up”

Genesis 2:9 continues the description of God's preparation of Eden.

The Hebrew verb is:

**וַיַּצְמַח (*wayyaṣmaḥ*)**

from:

**צָמַח (*ṣāmaḥ*)**

meaning:

**“to sprout,” “to grow,” “to cause to grow,” or “to make spring up.”**

The emphasis remains on divine initiative.

God does not merely place Adam into an already existing environment.

He causes the vegetation of the garden to grow.

The garden is therefore presented as a carefully prepared habitation.

The sequence of Genesis 2 is significant:

God forms the man.

God plants the garden.

God causes trees to grow.

God provides food.

God places the man in the garden.

The narrative continually emphasizes God's gracious provision.

---

## 2. “Every tree”

The expression:

**“every tree”**

emphasizes abundance.

Genesis 2:9 does not begin by presenting scarcity.

The garden contains a rich variety of trees.

The trees are both beautiful and useful.

They are:

**pleasant to the sight**

and

**good for food.**

The garden therefore provides both aesthetic delight and physical nourishment.

God's creation is not merely functional.

It is also beautiful.

God's provision is not merely about survival.

It includes goodness, abundance, and enjoyment.

---

## 3. “Pleasant to the sight”

The Hebrew expression is:

**נֶחְמָד לְמַרְאֶה (*neḥmād ləmarʾeh*)**

which can be rendered:

**“pleasant/desirable to the sight.”**

The verb/root associated with **נֶחְמָד (*neḥmād*)** carries the idea of something desirable or delightful.

The text therefore emphasizes the visual beauty of God's creation.

The trees are not merely edible.

They are attractive.

This is consistent with the repeated declaration in Genesis 1 that God's creation is good.

God's world possesses objective goodness and beauty because it comes from the Creator.

---

## 4. Beauty is part of God's good creation

Genesis 2:9 is important for a biblical theology of beauty.

Beauty is not presented as a sinful distraction.

The garden contains things that are deliberately described as pleasing to the eye.

God Himself creates an environment that includes visual delight.

This means that biblical theology does not require the rejection of beauty.

The problem arises when beauty is detached from God or becomes an object of sinful desire.

The good creation can be enjoyed rightly when it is received with gratitude and obedience to the Creator.

---

## 5. “Good for food”

The second description is:

**“good for food.”**

The Hebrew expression is:

**וְטוֹב לְמַאֲכָל (*wəṭôḇ ləmaʾăḵāl*)**

meaning:

**“and good for food.”**

The language connects directly with Genesis 1:29, where God gives vegetation and fruit-bearing plants as food.

The garden therefore continues God's provision for humanity.

Food is part of God's original blessing.

Eating is not inherently sinful.

Human beings were created to receive God's provision through the created order.

The problem in Genesis 3 is not that Adam and Eve eat.

The problem is that they eat in disobedience to God's command.

---

## 6. Beauty and usefulness together

The wording of Genesis 2:9 presents two complementary qualities:

**pleasant to the sight**

and

**good for food.**

The trees are beautiful and useful.

They satisfy both aesthetic and physical dimensions of human life.

This provides a picture of harmonious creation.

What is beautiful is also beneficial.

What provides nourishment is also delightful.

The Creator's provision is integrated rather than fragmented.

This is one reason Genesis 2 presents Eden as a picture of flourishing.

---

## 7. The tree of life

The verse then introduces a specific tree:

**“The tree of life was in the midst of the garden.”**

The Hebrew is:

**עֵץ הַחַיִּים (*ʿēṣ haḥayyîm*)**

literally:

**“tree of the life.”**

The tree of life is introduced without explanation.

The narrative assumes that its presence is meaningful.

Its full significance becomes clearer as the biblical story develops.

Genesis 3:22–24 shows that access to the tree is denied after humanity's rebellion.

Proverbs uses “tree of life” language metaphorically.

Revelation 2:7 and 22:1–5 return to the tree of life in the context of final restoration.

Thus the tree of life forms an important canonical connection between Genesis and Revelation.

---

## 8. “In the midst of the garden”

The location of the tree is emphasized:

**“in the midst of the garden.”**

The Hebrew phrase is:

**בְּתוֹךְ הַגָּן (*bəṯôḵ ha-gan*)**

meaning:

**“in the midst of the garden.”**

The tree occupies a central location within Eden.

This prepares the reader for the importance of the trees in the narrative.

The garden is not simply described as containing vegetation.

Two particular trees receive special attention.

Their presence becomes central to the moral and theological drama of Genesis 2–3.

---

## 9. The tree of the knowledge of good and evil

The second special tree is:

**“the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.”**

The Hebrew expression is:

**עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע (*ʿēṣ haddaʿaṯ ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*)**

literally:

**“the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.”**

The word **דַּעַת (*daʿaṯ*)** means:

**“knowledge.”**

The phrase **טוֹב וָרָע (*ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*)** means:

**“good and evil.”**

The significance of this expression must be interpreted carefully.

It does not necessarily mean merely intellectual information about good and evil.

In biblical usage, “knowing” can involve experiential knowledge, discernment, recognition, or a comprehensive grasp of something.

The narrative will demonstrate that humanity's desire for this knowledge is connected with a challenge to God's authority.

---

## 10. The two trees

Genesis 2:9 introduces two trees that will become central to the story:

**the tree of life**

and

**the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.**

They are both located within the garden.

The first is associated with life.

The second becomes associated with humanity's forbidden act.

Their juxtaposition prepares the reader for the command in Genesis 2:16–17.

Adam is permitted to eat from the trees of the garden, but he is prohibited from eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

Thus Genesis 2:9 provides the narrative setting for the test of obedience.

---

## 11. Divine generosity precedes prohibition

One of the most important theological observations is the order of the narrative.

Genesis 2:9 presents abundant provision.

Genesis 2:16 will say that Adam may freely eat from every tree of the garden.

Only then does God establish the prohibition.

The prohibition is therefore surrounded by generosity.

God is not portrayed as withholding everything from humanity.

He gives abundantly and restricts only one thing.

This becomes important when the serpent later suggests that God's command is fundamentally restrictive.

Genesis itself presents a different picture.

God is generous.

The prohibition is specific.

The garden is abundant.

---

## 12. The goodness of God's command

Genesis 2:9 prepares us to understand God's command in Genesis 2:16–17 as good.

God provides:

**beauty,**

**food,**

**life,**

and

**fellowship.**

The command establishes the boundary within which Adam is to enjoy these gifts.

The prohibition is therefore not evidence that creation is defective.

It is an expression of God's authority and wisdom.

The creature is free to enjoy God's provision but must remain under God's word.

---

## 13. The tree of knowledge and human autonomy

The precise meaning of the “knowledge of good and evil” has been interpreted in several ways.

Possible dimensions include:

**moral discernment,**

**experiential knowledge,**

**wisdom associated with maturity,**

or

**the assumption of authority to determine good and evil.**

The narrative context strongly emphasizes the last dimension as an important aspect of the temptation.

The serpent's promise in Genesis 3:5 concerns becoming like God, knowing good and evil.

The temptation therefore involves more than acquiring information.

It involves humanity's desire to grasp something that belongs properly to God's authority.

The fundamental problem is not knowledge itself.

It is knowledge pursued through rebellion.

---

## 14. Knowledge is not inherently evil

The tree should not be interpreted as teaching that knowledge itself is sinful.

Scripture consistently values wisdom, understanding, and knowledge of God.

Proverbs repeatedly commands the pursuit of wisdom.

The problem in Genesis 3 is not learning.

The problem is disobedient autonomy.

Adam and Eve seek something outside God's appointed boundary.

Thus Genesis 2–3 should not be used to argue that ignorance is spiritually superior to knowledge.

Rather, the text warns against seeking wisdom independently of God's revealed will.

---

## 15. The tree and God's lordship

The command concerning the tree establishes a basic Creator-creature distinction.

God determines what Adam may eat.

Adam does not determine morality for himself.

God's word defines the boundary.

This becomes one of the central issues of biblical theology.

Who has the authority to define good and evil?

Genesis answers:

**the Creator.**

Human beings are called to receive God's moral order rather than invent their own.

The Fall therefore represents not merely the breaking of a food restriction but rebellion against divine authority.

---

## 16. The tree and covenant obedience

Genesis 2:9 provides the physical setting for Adam's obedience.

The tree becomes the object around which the command is articulated.

Adam's relationship with God includes trust expressed through obedience.

He must believe that God's prohibition is good even when the forbidden fruit appears desirable.

The test therefore concerns faith.

Will Adam trust God's wisdom?

Or will he trust his own judgment?

The temptation in Genesis 3 answers the question tragically.

---

## 17. The tree and temptation

Genesis 3:6 later describes the woman seeing that the tree was:

**good for food,**

**a delight to the eyes,**

and

**desirable to make one wise.**

These descriptions deliberately echo Genesis 2:9.

Genesis 2:9 says the trees are:

**pleasant to the sight**

and

**good for food.**

Genesis 3:6 then describes the forbidden tree using similar language.

This literary connection is significant.

The same good qualities that characterize God's creation become incorporated into the temptation.

The problem is not that the tree is ugly or poisonous.

The problem is that God has forbidden its use.

---

## 18. The Fall twists God's good gifts

Genesis 2:9 therefore helps explain the nature of the Fall.

Sin does not require that God's creation be bad.

Sin takes something good and seeks to possess or use it contrary to God's word.

Food is good.

Beauty is good.

Knowledge is good.

But all three become distorted when pursued outside God's command.

This principle extends throughout Scripture.

Created gifts become idols when creatures seek them apart from the Creator.

---

## 19. The tree of life and immortality

Genesis 3:22 suggests that eating from the tree of life would allow the fallen human being to live continually.

After the Fall, God prevents access to it.

This demonstrates that immortality in the fallen condition would not be a blessing.

Humanity must not live forever in a state of corruption and rebellion.

God's judgment therefore contains a merciful dimension.

The expulsion from Eden prevents fallen humanity from perpetuating its condition indefinitely.

The biblical hope is not endless life in a fallen state.

It is resurrection life in a renewed creation.

---

## 20. The tree of life and final restoration

The tree of life reappears prominently in Revelation.

Revelation 22 presents the river of the water of life flowing from God's throne and the tree of life growing beside it.

The tree bears fruit.

Its leaves are associated with healing.

Most importantly, the curse is removed.

The biblical story therefore moves from:

**access to the tree**

to

**loss of access**

to

**restored access.**

The final appearance of the tree of life demonstrates that God's redemptive purpose ultimately restores what sin disrupted.

---

## 21. Christ and the tree of life

The New Testament does not explicitly identify Jesus as the tree of life.

Nevertheless, the tree of life theme is deeply compatible with the biblical presentation of Christ as the giver of eternal life.

Jesus repeatedly teaches that eternal life is found in Him.

He calls Himself:

**the bread of life**

and promises resurrection life to those who believe in Him.

The tree of life in Genesis therefore establishes an important biblical category that reaches its fulfillment in the life God gives through Christ.

Christ does not merely restore access to an Edenic tree.

He gives the life to which the tree points.

---

## 22. Christ and the curse

The tree of knowledge is associated with humanity's disobedience and the entrance of the curse.

The New Testament presents Christ as bearing the curse on behalf of His people.

Galatians 3:13 declares that Christ became a curse for us.

The crucifixion therefore becomes part of the reversal of Eden's tragedy.

Adam reaches for what God forbids.

Christ willingly accepts suffering and death in obedience to God.

Adam's disobedience brings condemnation.

Christ's obedience brings justification and life.

The canonical contrast is profound.

---

## 23. The garden and Christ's obedience

The garden motif appears again in Christ's passion.

In Gethsemane, Jesus faces the suffering that lies before Him and submits to the Father's will.

The contrast with Eden is striking.

In Eden, humanity chooses its own will over God's command.

In Gethsemane, Christ submits His will to the Father.

This does not mean that Gethsemane is simply a repetition of Eden.

Rather, the two scenes contribute to the broader biblical contrast between Adam's disobedience and Christ's obedience.

---

## 24. Genesis 2:9 and biblical anthropology

This verse also reveals something about humanity.

Human beings were created to enjoy God's provision.

They were created with appetites.

They were created with aesthetic awareness.

They were created within a moral order.

They were created to receive God's gifts according to God's word.

Therefore biblical anthropology is neither anti-body nor anti-pleasure.

Human desire belongs within God's design.

The problem is disordered desire.

---

## 25. Desire and obedience

The language of Genesis 2:9 prepares for Genesis 3's theology of desire.

The trees are pleasant.

The fruit is good.

The creation is desirable.

But desire must be governed by God's command.

The fact that something is attractive does not establish that it is permissible.

The fact that something appears beneficial does not establish that God has authorized it.

This is a fundamental biblical principle.

God's word determines the boundaries of legitimate human desire.

---

## 26. The garden as a place of testing

Eden is simultaneously:

**a place of blessing**

and

**a place of testing.**

God gives Adam extraordinary provision.

But Adam must trust God's command.

This demonstrates that blessing does not eliminate the need for obedience.

Indeed, abundance can make obedience particularly significant.

Adam has everything necessary for life.

He must simply trust God's word concerning what has been forbidden.

---

## 27. The literary role of Genesis 2:9

Genesis 2:9 performs several literary functions.

It expands the description of Eden.

It introduces the garden's abundant vegetation.

It emphasizes beauty and food.

It introduces the tree of life.

It introduces the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

It prepares for God's command in verses 16–17.

It anticipates the temptation of Genesis 3.

It establishes imagery that later returns in Revelation.

Thus this verse is not merely botanical information.

It is a major theological hinge.

---

## 28. Eden as the beginning of biblical sanctuary imagery

The centrality of the garden and the presence of the tree of life contribute to the sanctuary-like character of Eden.

Later Scripture repeatedly uses garden imagery in relation to God's presence.

The tabernacle and temple contain imagery associated with trees, flowers, cherubim, and God's dwelling.

The final vision of Revelation combines temple and garden imagery.

This suggests that Eden provides an important early pattern for understanding God's desire to dwell with humanity.

Again, this should be treated as a canonical theological pattern rather than claiming that Genesis explicitly calls Eden a temple.

---

## 29. The garden and sacred space

Eden can be described as sacred space in the sense that it is the special setting of God's presence and human service.

Adam's responsibility includes keeping the garden.

The entrance becomes guarded by cherubim after the Fall.

These features resemble later biblical patterns in which access to God's holy presence is regulated.

The theme of sacred space therefore begins very early in Scripture.

The ultimate goal is not merely sacred geography but restored fellowship with God.

---

## 30. Theological significance of the ground

Genesis 2:9 repeats the theme of the ground:

**“out of the ground…”**

The same ground from which Adam was formed produces the trees.

This creates a profound unity between humanity and creation.

Adam is formed from the ground.

The garden grows from the ground.

Adam eats from the ground's produce.

Adam works the ground.

After the Fall, the ground is cursed.

Humanity's relationship with the earth is therefore central to Genesis's theology of creation.

---

## 31. Creation as gift

Genesis 2:9 demonstrates that creation is received before it is exploited.

God creates.

God provides.

Humanity receives.

This is the foundation of stewardship.

The world is not ultimately humanity's possession.

It is God's gift.

Therefore the proper response to creation is gratitude and responsible use.

The biblical doctrine of creation leads naturally toward stewardship.

---

## 32. Food and communion

Food occupies an important role in the biblical narrative.

In Eden, food is provided by God.

Later Scripture repeatedly uses meals in covenantal and communal contexts.

The Passover meal commemorates redemption.

Jesus establishes the Lord's Supper.

The Bible concludes with imagery of the marriage supper of the Lamb.

Genesis 2:9 therefore begins a long biblical association between God's provision and eating.

Food can be a sign of blessing and fellowship.

But Genesis 3 demonstrates that eating can also become the occasion of rebellion when God's word is rejected.

---

## 33. Beauty, goodness, and God's character

The trees are beautiful and good.

This reflects the Creator's character.

God is the source of goodness.

The created order reflects His wisdom and generosity.

Genesis does not present beauty as an autonomous standard independent of God.

Beauty belongs within creation because God made it.

The proper response to beauty is therefore ultimately worship.

Creation should lead the creature toward the Creator rather than replacing Him.

---

## 34. The tree of knowledge and wisdom

The phrase “knowledge of good and evil” should also be distinguished from the biblical concept of wisdom.

Scripture encourages people to seek wisdom.

But biblical wisdom begins with the fear of the LORD.

True wisdom is not autonomous mastery.

It is submission to God's truth.

Genesis 3 shows the danger of seeking wisdom apart from God's command.

The tree therefore becomes a symbol of humanity's temptation to determine reality on its own terms.

---

## 35. The Creator defines good and evil

A fundamental theological principle emerges from the passage:

**good and evil are not ultimately defined by autonomous human preference.**

God establishes the moral order.

The creature receives it.

This is foundational to biblical ethics.

The Fall is therefore epistemological as well as moral.

Humanity rejects God's word and attempts to establish its own authority.

The serpent's promise of becoming “like God” exposes the deeper issue.

Humanity wants God's position without dependence upon God.

---

## 36. Genesis 2:9 and sin

Sin can therefore be understood as more than breaking isolated rules.

At its deepest level, sin is rebellion against God's authority.

The tree provides the concrete test.

The temptation challenges God's word.

The human response rejects God's boundary.

The result is alienation, shame, judgment, and death.

Genesis 2:9 prepares the reader to understand that tragedy.

The tree is good.

The garden is good.

God's provision is good.

The evil enters through human rebellion.

---

## 37. The tree of life and eternal life

The tree of life also establishes an important biblical category for eternal life.

Humanity does not possess autonomous immortality.

Continued life depends upon God's provision.

The tree symbolizes continued access to life within God's created order.

After the Fall, access is removed.

The gospel therefore does not teach that humans naturally possess eternal life independent of God.

Eternal life is God's gift.

The New Testament makes this explicit in its teaching about eternal life through Christ.

---

## 38. Genesis 2:9 and resurrection hope

The loss of access to the tree of life creates a problem that Scripture eventually resolves through redemption.

Humanity dies.

Christ dies.

Christ rises.

Believers receive resurrection life.

The final restoration includes the tree of life.

This is not accidental symbolism.

The biblical story moves from death toward life.

The final victory is not simply the recovery of Adam's original condition.

It is the consummation of God's purposes in resurrection and new creation.

---

## 39. The canonical movement

Genesis 2:9 can therefore be placed within a larger biblical movement:

**Creation**

→ God provides life and abundance.

**Fall**

→ humanity rebels and loses access to the tree of life.

**Redemption**

→ Christ bears sin and defeats death.

**Resurrection**

→ Christ inaugurates the new creation.

**Consummation**

→ the tree of life appears again and the curse is removed.

The verse is therefore foundational for biblical theology.

---

## 40. Genesis 2:9 and the whole biblical enterprise

Genesis 2:9 introduces themes that recur throughout Scripture:

**life,**

**food,**

**beauty,**

**wisdom,**

**obedience,**

**desire,**

**judgment,**

**curse,**

**redemption,**

and

**restoration.**

It also introduces two trees whose imagery extends all the way to Revelation.

The Bible's final vision is therefore not disconnected from Genesis.

The end of Scripture deliberately answers themes established near the beginning.

---

## 41. The central theological proposition

Genesis 2:9 can be summarized in this central proposition:

**The LORD God graciously provides an abundant and beautiful garden, placing within it the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thereby establishing the setting for human enjoyment, obedience, moral accountability, and the unfolding drama of life, death, and redemption.**

Or more simply:

**God's creation is abundant and good, but humanity must receive God's gifts within the boundaries established by God's word.**

---

## Exegetical Outline

**Genesis 2:9**

**“Out of the ground the LORD God made to spring up”**

→ God is the source of the garden's abundance.

→ Creation is dependent upon divine provision.

**“Every tree”**

→ Eden contains abundant variety.

→ God's provision is generous rather than sparse.

**“Pleasant to the sight”**

→ Beauty belongs to God's good creation.

→ Aesthetic delight is part of human enjoyment.

**“Good for food”**

→ God provides nourishment.

→ Food is part of the original goodness of creation.

**“The tree of life”**

→ Life is God's gift.

→ The tree anticipates the later biblical theme of eternal life and final restoration.

**“In the midst of the garden”**

→ The tree occupies a central place within Eden.

→ Its location prepares for the narrative significance of the garden.

**“The tree of the knowledge of good and evil”**

→ Humanity is placed under God's moral authority.

→ The coming prohibition establishes a test of trust and obedience.

**Central idea:**

**God abundantly provides beauty, nourishment, and life in Eden while establishing His authority over humanity, preparing the setting for the Fall and the Bible's later story of redemption and restored life.**

---

## Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:9 establishes or reinforces these major doctrines:

1. **Divine providence** — God provides the environment and resources necessary for human flourishing.
2. **Creation** — the material world is God's good creation.
3. **The goodness of creation** — beauty, food, and physical enjoyment belong to God's original design.
4. **Human dependence** — humanity receives its provisions from God.
5. **Stewardship** — created resources belong ultimately to God and are entrusted to humanity.
6. **Human responsibility** — God's gifts are to be enjoyed within God's commands.
7. **Moral authority** — God, not autonomous humanity, establishes the boundaries of good and evil.
8. **Human desire** — desire is part of creation but must remain subject to God's word.
9. **Human mortality** — continued life is dependent upon God.
10. **The tree of life** — life and immortality are ultimately divine gifts.
11. **The Fall** — sin involves rebellion against God's authority rather than a defect in creation.
12. **Original sin** — Adam's disobedience becomes foundational to humanity's fallen condition.
13. **Christ as the Last Adam** — Christ's obedience stands in contrast to Adam's disobedience.
14. **Eternal life** — true life is ultimately found in God's saving work.
15. **Resurrection** — redemption culminates in restored and transformed life.
16. **New creation** — the tree of life imagery reaches its canonical fulfillment in Revelation.
17. **God's dwelling presence** — Eden establishes an early biblical pattern of God dwelling with humanity.
18. **Redemptive history** — Genesis 2:9 introduces themes that unfold throughout the whole biblical narrative.

---

## A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

Genesis 2:9 can be structured around three propositions:

### I. GOD PROVIDES ABUNDANTLY

**“Every tree that is pleasant to the sight and good for food.”**

God's creation is generous, beautiful, and sufficient.

### II. GOD GIVES LIFE

**“The tree of life was in the midst of the garden.”**

Life ultimately belongs to God and is received as His gift.

### III. GOD ESTABLISHES MORAL BOUNDARIES

**“The tree of the knowledge of good and evil.”**

Humanity is free to enjoy God's provision but must remain under God's authority.

**Main proposition:**

**Genesis 2:9 teaches that God graciously provides humanity with a beautiful and abundant creation while establishing Himself as the sovereign giver of life and the rightful authority over human desire and morality.**

The canonical movement can be summarized:

**Genesis 1:29**  
God gives vegetation for food.

**Genesis 2:8–9**  
God establishes Eden and provides beautiful, nourishing trees.

**Genesis 2:16–17**  
God gives Adam abundant permission together with one prohibition.

**Genesis 3:1–7**  
The serpent challenges God's word, and humanity disobeys.

**Genesis 3:22–24**  
Humanity loses access to the tree of life.

**Proverbs 3:18**  
Wisdom is described as a tree of life to those who lay hold of her.

**John 6:35–40**  
Jesus presents Himself as the bread of life and the giver of resurrection life.

**Romans 5:12–21**  
Adam's trespass brings condemnation and death; Christ's obedience brings righteousness and life.

**1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49**  
Adam is contrasted with Christ, the Last Adam, in relation to death and resurrection.

**Galatians 3:13**  
Christ bears the curse on behalf of His people.

**Revelation 2:7**  
The one who conquers is promised access to the tree of life in God's paradise.

**Revelation 22:1–5**  
The river and tree of life appear in the renewed creation, and the curse is removed.

Genesis 2:9 is therefore a pivotal verse in the theology of creation and redemption.

God creates a world that is beautiful.

God provides food.

God gives life.

God establishes moral boundaries.

The tragedy of the Fall does not originate in a bad creation or an ungenerous Creator.

It originates in humanity's rejection of God's word.

The tree of the knowledge of good and evil becomes the point at which humanity attempts to grasp moral autonomy.

The tree of life, meanwhile, becomes the great biblical symbol of the life that humanity loses through sin and that God ultimately restores.

The biblical story therefore moves from the garden to exile, from death to resurrection, and from the loss of the tree of life to its restoration in the new creation.

In Christ, the Last Adam, God accomplishes what fallen humanity could never accomplish by its own wisdom or obedience.

The One who enters the fallen world in obedience bears the curse, defeats death, and inaugurates the life of the age to come.

Thus Genesis 2:9 is not merely a description of two remarkable trees.

It establishes the theological landscape in which the biblical drama of creation, command, temptation, fall, judgment, redemption, resurrection, and restoration unfolds.

**The God who planted the garden is the God who gives life; the Creator who established the boundary is the God whose wisdom humanity must trust; and the God who guarded the tree of life after the Fall is the God who ultimately restores life through Christ in the renewed creation.**
