# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:4

## Genesis 2:4

> **“These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens.”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**אֵלֶּה תוֹלְדוֹת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְהָאָרֶץ בְּהִבָּרְאָם בְּיוֹם עֲשׂוֹת יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים אֶרֶץ וְשָׁמָיִם**

## Transliteration

*ʾēlleh tôlədôt haššāmayim wəhāʾāreṣ bəhibbārəʾām bə-yôm ʿăśôt YHWH ʾĕlōhîm ʾereṣ wəšāmayim*

## Literal Rendering

**“These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day of the LORD God making earth and heavens.”**

---

## 1. “These are the generations” — אֵלֶּה תוֹלְדוֹת (*ʾēlleh tôlədôt*)

Genesis 2:4 begins with one of the most important structural formulas in the book of Genesis:

**אֵלֶּה תוֹלְדוֹת (*ʾēlleh tôlədôt*)**

Literally:

**“These are the generations…”**

The Hebrew word **תּוֹלְדוֹת (*tôlədôt*)** comes from the root **יָלַד (*yālad*)**, associated with **bringing forth, begetting, or generating**.

The expression can therefore refer to:

**generations, descendants, accounts, or the history/development associated with a particular person or subject.**

The formula appears repeatedly throughout Genesis.

For example:

**“These are the generations of Noah…”**

**“These are the generations of the sons of Noah…”**

**“These are the generations of Shem…”**

**“These are the generations of Terah…”**

The phrase is therefore one of the major literary devices used to organize the book.

Genesis 2:4 introduces a new major section of the narrative.

The creation account has reached its conclusion in Genesis 2:1–3.

Now the narrative turns toward the story of humanity in the created world.

---

## 2. “Of the heavens and the earth” — הַשָּׁמַיִם וְהָאָרֶץ (*haššāmayim wəhāʾāreṣ*)

The subject of this *tôlədôt* formula is:

**“the heavens and the earth.”**

This deliberately recalls Genesis 1:1:

**“In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.”**

The connection is important.

Genesis 1 begins with:

**the creation of the heavens and the earth.**

Genesis 2:4 now introduces:

**the generations/account of the heavens and the earth.**

The reader is therefore moving from the **creation of the world** to the **history that unfolds within that world**.

Genesis is not merely interested in explaining how the universe came into existence.

It is also concerned with what happened after creation.

The remainder of Genesis 2 will narrow the focus to humanity, Eden, marriage, God's command, the serpent, and the Fall.

---

## 3. The *tôlədôt* formula as a structural marker

The expression **“these are the generations”** functions as a major structural marker throughout Genesis.

Rather than treating Genesis as a collection of disconnected stories, the *tôlədôt* formulas demonstrate that the book has a carefully organized literary structure.

The formula appears to introduce material that develops the history associated with the preceding name or subject.

This creates a significant interpretative observation:

**Genesis 2:4 is not merely repeating Genesis 1.**

It is transitioning the reader into a new narrative development.

Genesis 1:1–2:3 gives the cosmic creation account.

Genesis 2:4 onward focuses upon the human story within that creation.

The scope becomes narrower.

The subject becomes more personal.

The cosmic order gives way to the garden.

The garden gives way to humanity.

Humanity gives way to Adam and Eve.

And the narrative eventually leads to the entrance of sin.

---

## 4. “When they were created” — בְּהִבָּרְאָם (*bəhibbārəʾām*)

The phrase:

**בְּהִבָּרְאָם (*bəhibbārəʾām*)**

means:

**“when they were created.”**

The verb derives from **בָּרָא (*bārāʾ*)**, the important creation verb repeatedly used in Genesis 1.

The form is passive:

**“when they were created.”**

The heavens and earth are therefore presented as the result of divine creation.

The statement looks backward to the creation account that has just concluded.

Genesis 2:4 thus deliberately links the coming narrative to Genesis 1.

The world of Genesis 2 is the world God created in Genesis 1.

There are not two different creations.

The text presents a unified creation account followed by a more focused narrative concerning humanity.

---

## 5. Genesis 2:4 and the unity of Genesis 1–2

Genesis 2:4 is particularly important for understanding the relationship between Genesis 1 and Genesis 2.

Some readers have treated Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 as competing creation accounts.

But Genesis 2:4 itself provides a strong literary connection.

The verse explicitly refers back to:

**“the heavens and the earth when they were created.”**

Then it begins describing events within that created order.

Genesis 1 gives a broad, structured account of creation.

Genesis 2 provides a more focused account of humanity and Eden.

The movement can therefore be described as:

**cosmic creation → human creation → Eden → command → marriage → temptation → Fall.**

Genesis 2 does not need to repeat every event of Genesis 1.

It narrows the camera.

---

## 6. “In the day” — בְּיוֹם (*bə-yôm*)

The phrase:

**בְּיוֹם (*bə-yôm*)**

literally means:

**“in the day”** or **“when.”**

Here it functions as a temporal expression referring to the period in which the LORD God made the earth and heavens.

This expression should not automatically be interpreted as referring to a single twenty-four-hour day.

The immediate grammatical construction can function idiomatically as:

**“when God made…”**

The emphasis is on the time or occasion of God's creative activity.

The verse is therefore summarizing the creation work while transitioning into the next narrative section.

---

## 7. “The LORD God” — יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים (*YHWH ʾĕlōhîm*)

One of the most significant features of Genesis 2:4 is the divine name:

**יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים (*YHWH ʾĕlōhîm*)**

translated:

**“the LORD God.”**

Genesis 1 predominantly uses:

**אֱלֹהִים (*ʾĕlōhîm*) — “God.”**

Beginning in Genesis 2:4, the name **YHWH** becomes prominent alongside **Elohim**.

This is not a different deity.

It is the same God.

The combination emphasizes both God's sovereign creative identity and His personal covenantal identity.

**Elohim** emphasizes God as the powerful Creator.

**YHWH** is God's personal covenant name, especially significant in His relationship with His people.

Genesis 2 therefore moves from the cosmic Creator toward the personal God who forms, commands, places, speaks, and relates to humanity.

---

## 8. The significance of YHWH in Genesis 2

The use of **YHWH** is especially meaningful because the narrative is about relationship.

In Genesis 1, the repeated formula is:

**“And God said…”**

God commands creation.

In Genesis 2, the LORD God:

**forms man,**

**plants a garden,**

**places man in the garden,**

**gives a command,**

**brings animals to Adam,**

**creates the woman,**

and

**establishes marriage.**

The narrative therefore emphasizes God's personal involvement with humanity.

The Creator is not distant.

He is the God who personally orders the human environment and gives humanity a vocation and command.

---

## 9. “The LORD God made” — עֲשׂוֹת (*ʿăśôt*)

The verb **עָשָׂה (*ʿāśâ*)** means:

**“to make,” “to do,” or “to accomplish.”**

Genesis 2:4 therefore uses another major creation verb.

Genesis 1 has already used:

**בָּרָא (*bārāʾ*) — create**

and

**עָשָׂה (*ʿāśâ*) — make/do.**

The verse therefore summarizes God's creative activity using both concepts.

God created the heavens and earth.

God made the earth and heavens.

The language emphasizes divine agency.

The world is God's work.

---

## 10. “The earth and the heavens” — אֶרֶץ וְשָׁמָיִם

Notice that the order at the end of Genesis 2:4 is:

**“the earth and the heavens.”**

Genesis 1:1 has:

**“the heavens and the earth.”**

The reversal is literary rather than contradictory.

Genesis 1 moves from the cosmic whole toward the earth and its ordering.

Genesis 2 begins from the earth as the setting for the human story.

The focus has now shifted.

The reader is about to enter the garden of Eden.

The earth becomes the immediate stage upon which the human drama unfolds.

The narrative therefore moves from:

**cosmos → earth → garden → man and woman.**

---

## 11. Genesis 2:4 as a transition from creation to covenant history

Genesis 2:4 marks a major transition.

Genesis 1–2:3 answers:

**Who created the world?**

Genesis 2:4 onward begins answering:

**What happened to the world God created?**

The coming narrative introduces:

human vocation,

divine command,

marriage,

temptation,

disobedience,

judgment,

and the consequences of sin.

The creation account therefore provides the theological foundation for everything that follows.

Without Genesis 1–2, the Fall of Genesis 3 would have no proper context.

Sin is rebellion against the Creator's good order.

---

## 12. The created order and human responsibility

Because Genesis 2:4 identifies the world as God's creation, human beings are not autonomous owners of it.

They are creatures within God's world.

The next verses will describe Adam's placement in Eden and his responsibility to work and keep the garden.

The theological order is important:

**God creates → God establishes order → God gives humanity responsibility.**

Human responsibility therefore derives from God's authority.

Human beings receive vocation from the Creator.

They do not invent their ultimate purpose independently of Him.

---

## 13. Genesis 2:4 and the goodness of creation

The world described in Genesis 2 remains the good creation of Genesis 1.

Before sin enters the narrative, there is no indication that the material world itself is defective.

God has created the world.

God has ordered the world.

God provides a garden.

God provides food.

God creates companionship.

God establishes marriage.

God gives humanity meaningful work.

Everything is situated within God's good design.

This becomes essential for interpreting Genesis 3.

The problem is not God's creation.

The problem is human rebellion.

---

## 14. Genesis 2:4 and the doctrine of God

The verse provides a rich presentation of God.

He is:

**Creator** — He creates the heavens and earth.

**Sovereign** — He determines the order of creation.

**Personal** — He is identified as YHWH.

**Powerful** — He is Elohim.

**Purposeful** — He makes and orders the world.

**Relational** — He will interact directly with humanity.

The combination **YHWH Elohim** therefore prepares the reader for a God who is both transcendent Creator and personal Lord.

---

## 15. Genesis 2:4 and the doctrine of humanity

Although the verse itself does not yet describe Adam in detail, it establishes the context in which biblical anthropology must be understood.

Humanity exists within a created world.

Humanity is not self-originating.

Humanity does not define reality.

Humanity receives existence and vocation from God.

The next section will therefore describe humanity from the standpoint of God's creative authority.

This provides the foundation for the biblical understanding of human dignity and responsibility.

Human beings possess extraordinary dignity because they are God's creatures made in His image.

But they also possess creaturely limitations because they are not God.

---

## 16. Genesis 2:4 and marriage

The verse itself does not yet mention marriage, but it introduces the narrative that will establish the foundation for biblical marriage theology.

Genesis 2 will explain that God created woman for man and that the two become one flesh.

Thus Genesis 2:4 serves as the doorway into one of Scripture's foundational passages concerning human relationships.

Marriage is therefore presented within the context of God's created order rather than as a merely human social invention.

---

## 17. Genesis 2:4 and Christ

Genesis 2:4 does not explicitly mention Christ.

Nevertheless, the New Testament places Christ within the Creator identity.

John 1:3 teaches that all things were made through the Word.

Colossians 1:16 teaches that all things were created through Christ and for Christ.

Hebrews 1:2 identifies the Son as the One through whom God made the world.

Therefore, the God who creates and orders the world in Genesis belongs to the fuller canonical revelation in which the Son participates in creation.

The connection should be made canonically rather than by claiming that Genesis 2:4 explicitly names Jesus.

Genesis establishes the Creator.

The New Testament reveals the Son's participation in that creative work.

---

## 18. Genesis 2:4 and the biblical enterprise

Genesis 2:4 is also important for the whole biblical narrative because the story that follows will eventually explain why redemption is necessary.

The Bible's major storyline can be traced through:

**Creation**

→ **Fall**

→ **Redemption**

→ **Consummation.**

Genesis 2:4 stands at the transition from the first stage toward the human story that will produce the crisis of the Fall.

The God who made the world will be revealed as the God against whom humanity rebels.

And the same biblical story will ultimately reveal God's redemptive answer in Christ.

---

## 19. Literary function within Genesis

Genesis 2:4 functions as a major **literary hinge**.

It concludes the broad creation account while introducing the focused account of humanity and Eden.

The verse can therefore be viewed in two directions.

Looking backward:

**It summarizes the creation of the heavens and earth.**

Looking forward:

**It introduces the narrative of human life within that creation.**

This makes Genesis 2:4 one of the most important transitional verses in the early chapters of Genesis.

It moves the reader from:

**“God created…”**

to:

**“the LORD God made…”**

and from the cosmic creation account to the intimate human narrative.

---

## 20. The central theological proposition

Genesis 2:4 can be summarized in this central proposition:

**The heavens and earth were created by the LORD God, and the history that follows unfolds within the ordered world He made.**

Or more simply:

**The God who created the world is the personal Lord who governs the human story within it.**

The verse therefore establishes the setting for everything that follows.

Humanity's story is God's story of creation, command, rebellion, judgment, grace, and ultimately redemption.

---

## Exegetical Outline

**Genesis 2:4**

**“These are the generations”**

→ Introduces a major literary section in Genesis.

→ Signals the development of the history associated with the preceding subject.

**“of the heavens and the earth”**

→ Recalls Genesis 1:1.

→ Identifies the created order as the setting of the coming narrative.

**“when they were created”**

→ Affirms divine creation.

→ Connects Genesis 2 with Genesis 1.

**“in the day”**

→ Introduces the temporal setting of God's creative work.

→ Functions naturally as “when” in this context.

**“the LORD God”**

→ Combines God's personal covenant name with the title of divine Creator.

→ Emphasizes both sovereignty and personal relationship.

**“made the earth and the heavens”**

→ Reaffirms divine agency.

→ Shifts the narrative focus toward the earth and humanity.

**Central idea:**

**The LORD God created the heavens and earth, and Genesis now turns from the cosmic account of creation to the unfolding human history within God's good creation.**

---

## Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:4 establishes or reinforces these major doctrines:

1. **Creation** — the heavens and earth originate from God's creative work.
2. **Divine sovereignty** — God rules the world He created.
3. **Creator–creature distinction** — humanity exists within God's created order.
4. **Divine personal identity** — the Creator is also YHWH, the personal Lord.
5. **Biblical anthropology** — humanity must be understood within God's created purpose.
6. **Human accountability** — creatures are responsible to their Creator.
7. **Creation's goodness** — the narrative that follows begins within God's good created order.
8. **Marriage and human relationships** — Genesis 2 provides the foundation for later biblical teaching.
9. **Biblical authority** — God's commands arise from His authority as Creator.
10. **Canonical unity** — Genesis 2 develops rather than contradicts Genesis 1.
11. **Christological fulfillment** — the New Testament identifies the Son as active in creation.
12. **Redemptive history** — the human story that follows prepares the way for the biblical account of the Fall and redemption.

---

## A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

Genesis 2:4 can be structured around three propositions:

### I. GOD CREATED THE WORLD

**“These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created…”**

The world is not self-existent.

It is the product of God's creative work.

### II. GOD IS THE PERSONAL LORD OF CREATION

**“…the LORD God…”**

The Creator is not an impersonal force.

He is the personal Lord who relates to His creatures.

### III. HUMAN HISTORY UNFOLDS WITHIN GOD'S CREATION

**“…made the earth and the heavens.”**

The coming story of Adam and Eve must be understood within God's created order.

Humanity's choices occur in a world that already belongs to God.

**Main proposition:**

**Genesis 2:4 teaches that the LORD God created the heavens and earth and now turns the narrative toward the human history that unfolds within His good and sovereignly ordered creation.**

The canonical movement can be summarized:

**Genesis 1:1–2:3**  
God creates the heavens and earth.

**Genesis 2:4–25**  
God establishes humanity within creation.

**Genesis 3:1–24**  
Humanity rebels against the Creator.

**Genesis 12:1–3**  
God begins the redemptive covenantal movement through Abraham.

**John 1:1–3**  
The Word is revealed as active in creation.

**Colossians 1:16–17**  
All things are created through Christ and for Christ.

**Revelation 21:1–5**  
God brings His purposes to consummation in the renewed creation.

Genesis 2:4 therefore stands as a major doorway in the biblical narrative.

The creation account has concluded.

The human story is about to begin.

The God who created the heavens and earth will now reveal Himself as the LORD who forms humanity, establishes covenantal boundaries, gives vocation, provides companionship, confronts rebellion, and ultimately unfolds His redemptive purpose.

**The Creator of the world is also the Lord of the history that unfolds within it.**
