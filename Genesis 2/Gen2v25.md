# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:25

## Genesis 2:25

> **“And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed.”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּהְיוּ שְׁנֵיהֶם עֲרוּמִּים הָאָדָם וְאִשְׁתּוֹ וְלֹא יִתְבֹּשָׁשׁוּ**

## Transliteration

*Vayyihyû šənêhem ʿărummîm, hāʾādām wəʾištô, wəlōʾ yitbōšāšû.*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the two of them were naked, the man and his wife, and they were not ashamed.”**

---

## 1. The Verse in Its Immediate Context

Genesis 2:25 concludes the creation narrative of Genesis 2 and provides the final description of the man and his wife before the entrance of sin in Genesis 3. The preceding verses have described God's creation of the woman, Adam's joyful recognition of her, and the creational institution of marriage. The man leaves his father and mother, holds fast to his wife, and the two become one flesh.

Verse 25 now describes the condition of the couple within that original relationship.

They are naked.

They are together.

And they are not ashamed.

This final statement is crucial because Genesis 3 will introduce the catastrophic change that follows human disobedience. The contrast between Genesis 2:25 and Genesis 3:7 is therefore essential for understanding the biblical theology of innocence, shame, sin, sexuality, and human relationships.

---

## 2. “And the Man and His Wife”

The verse identifies the pair as **the man and his wife**.

The relationship established in verses 22–24 is now explicitly described as marital.

The woman is no longer merely “Woman.”

She is the man's wife.

The text therefore closes the chapter by affirming the completed marital relationship established by God.

---

## 3. “The Two of Them”

The Hebrew expression emphasizes the two persons together.

They are distinct individuals.

Yet they are united as husband and wife.

This corresponds directly with Genesis 2:24: “they shall become one flesh.”

The language therefore maintains both personal distinction and marital unity.

---

## 4. “Were Naked”

The Hebrew adjective **עֲרוּמִּים (*ʿărummîm*)** means “naked.”

The statement is straightforward.

Adam and his wife possess no clothing.

There is nothing hidden from one another.

Their physical bodies are completely exposed.

Yet the important point is not merely their lack of clothing.

The text tells us how they experience that condition.

They are not ashamed.

---

## 5. Nakedness Before the Fall

The couple's nakedness exists before sin.

This means that the human body itself is not presented as inherently shameful.

God created human beings as embodied creatures.

Their bodies are part of God's good creation.

Nakedness in this context is associated with innocence rather than guilt.

---

## 6. “And Were Not Ashamed”

The final phrase is the theological climax of the verse.

The Hebrew verb **בּוֹשׁ (*bôš*)** means “to be ashamed,” “to feel shame,” or “to be humiliated.”

The negative construction means that the man and woman experienced no shame in their nakedness.

Their relationship contains no guilt, fear, exploitation, or embarrassment.

---

## 7. The Meaning of Shame

Shame in Scripture can have several dimensions.

It may refer to humiliation, disgrace, exposure, guilt, or the painful awareness of wrongdoing.

Genesis 2:25 establishes a condition in which such shame is absent.

The man and woman possess nothing that they need to conceal from one another.

---

## 8. Innocence

Genesis 2:25 presents the couple in a state of innocence.

They have not yet sinned.

They have no reason to fear judgment from God or from one another.

Their openness is therefore completely unthreatening.

---

## 9. Transparency

The couple's nakedness illustrates complete transparency.

Nothing separates them physically.

Nothing separates them relationally.

There is no deception.

There is no concealment.

The marital relationship exists in openness and trust.

---

## 10. Trust

The absence of shame implies relational security.

Adam and his wife can see one another without fear.

They are completely exposed, yet they do not experience rejection.

The relationship is therefore characterized by trust.

---

## 11. The Goodness of the Human Body

The verse contributes to the Bible's positive theology of the human body.

The body is created by God.

The body is not inherently evil.

Physical existence is not a mistake.

Human sexuality is part of God's created order.

The problem introduced in Genesis 3 is sin, not embodiment itself.

---

## 12. The Goodness of Sexuality

Genesis 2 places sexuality within the good creation.

The man and woman are sexually differentiated.

They are married.

They are one flesh.

They are naked together.

And they are not ashamed.

This sequence demonstrates that sexual differentiation and marital intimacy belong to God's original design.

---

## 13. Nakedness and Marriage

The context is explicitly marital.

The verse does not present nakedness as a general social condition.

It describes the husband and wife.

Their nakedness belongs within the intimate relationship established in verse 24.

---

## 14. Nakedness Is Not the Same as Sexual Sin

The existence of nakedness does not itself constitute sin.

Genesis 2:25 occurs before the Fall.

The biblical problem is not the human body.

Sin distorts how human beings perceive and use their bodies.

The verse therefore prevents a simplistic equation between nakedness and moral evil.

---

## 15. The Absence of Lust

The text does not describe the man and woman as experiencing lust, exploitation, or objectification.

Their bodies are seen without sinful desire.

Their relationship is characterized by innocence.

This changes dramatically after the Fall.

---

## 16. The Absence of Exploitation

There is no indication of domination or manipulation.

The man and woman exist together in the goodness of God's creation.

Their physical vulnerability does not produce fear.

Their relationship is safe.

---

## 17. The Absence of Fear

Shame and fear often accompany exposure after sin.

Here neither appears.

The man and woman are exposed to one another and remain secure.

The verse therefore portrays the original human relationship as free from the fear produced by guilt.

---

## 18. Genesis 2:25 and Genesis 3:7

The contrast with Genesis 3:7 is essential.

After eating the forbidden fruit, Adam and Eve become aware of their nakedness and make coverings.

The same nakedness that previously produced no shame now becomes associated with shame.

The difference is not that their bodies suddenly became bad.

The difference is that sin has entered.

---

## 19. Sin Changes Human Perception

Before the Fall, the man and woman are naked and unashamed.

After the Fall, they see their nakedness as a source of shame.

The Fall therefore changes not merely their legal status before God but also their perception of themselves, each other, and their environment.

---

## 20. The Entrance of Shame

Genesis 3 introduces shame immediately after sin.

Adam and Eve hide from God.

They make coverings.

They become afraid.

The contrast demonstrates the relational consequences of disobedience.

---

## 21. Shame as a Sign of the Fall

Genesis 2:25 establishes the pre-Fall condition.

Genesis 3:7 describes the post-Fall condition.

The transition from “not ashamed” to awareness and concealment provides one of the clearest narrative indicators of what sin does to humanity.

---

## 22. Shame and Alienation

Sin produces alienation.

The man and woman become alienated from God.

They also experience a new form of vulnerability toward one another.

The openness of Genesis 2:25 is replaced by concealment.

---

## 23. The Theology of Covering

In Genesis 3:7, Adam and Eve make coverings for themselves.

This is the first human attempt to cover the consequences of sin.

Genesis 2:25 provides the contrast.

Originally, no covering was necessary.

After sin, concealment becomes necessary from their perspective.

---

## 24. Theological Implication: Creation Is Good

Genesis 2:25 confirms the goodness of creation.

Human bodies are good.

Marriage is good.

Sexual differentiation is good.

Marital intimacy is good.

The Fall does not reveal that creation was defective.

It reveals that humanity has rebelled against the Creator.

---

## 25. Theological Implication: Sin Is Not Embodiment

The biblical narrative does not identify the body as the source of evil.

Adam and Eve were embodied before sin.

They were naked before sin.

They were sexually differentiated before sin.

The problem is disobedience, not physical existence.

---

## 26. Theological Implication: Marriage Is a Safe Union

The original marital relationship contains no shame.

The husband and wife can be completely exposed to one another.

Their union is characterized by trust and security.

This provides the creational ideal toward which biblical marriage theology points.

---

## 27. Theological Implication: Human Relationships Were Originally Unbroken

There is no alienation between husband and wife.

There is no hiding.

There is no fear.

There is no accusation.

There is no shame.

The relationship is whole.

---

## 28. Theological Implication: Sin Corrupts Good Gifts

Marriage, sexuality, embodiment, and relational intimacy are good gifts.

Sin does not create these realities.

Sin corrupts them.

This distinction is important for Christian theology.

---

## 29. Theological Implication: Innocence and Holiness

The absence of shame reflects innocence.

The couple has not violated God's command.

They live within the boundaries established by their Creator.

Their innocence is therefore relationally and morally significant.

---

## 30. Theological Implication: Openness Before God

The man and woman are also living openly before God.

They have nothing to hide.

This prepares for the devastating reversal in Genesis 3.

After sin, they hide from the presence of God.

---

## 31. Theological Implication: Human Vulnerability

Nakedness represents complete vulnerability.

Before sin, vulnerability does not produce exploitation.

The man and woman are secure in God's good creation.

This demonstrates how deeply sin affects human relationships.

---

## 32. Interpretative Challenge: Is Nakedness Always Good?

No.

Genesis 2:25 describes a particular pre-Fall context.

The verse cannot be isolated from its marital and creational setting.

Later biblical passages establish appropriate boundaries concerning nakedness and sexuality.

---

## 33. Interpretative Challenge: Is the Verse Primarily Sexual?

Sexuality is certainly relevant.

However, the verse is also about innocence, trust, relational transparency, and the absence of shame.

Reducing it to sexuality alone misses the broader theological point.

---

## 34. Interpretative Challenge: Does Shame Come From the Body?

No.

The body was created good.

The appearance of shame in Genesis 3 follows disobedience.

The biblical narrative therefore locates the fundamental problem in sin rather than embodiment.

---

## 35. Interpretative Challenge: Why Mention Nakedness?

The statement prepares the reader for Genesis 3.

The contrast between “not ashamed” in 2:25 and the shame and covering in 3:7 is deliberate.

The verse establishes the baseline from which the Fall represents a dramatic departure.

---

## 36. Interpretative Challenge: Is This Only About Sexual Innocence?

No.

The broader condition includes innocence before God and openness between the man and woman.

Their relationship is completely unbroken.

---

## 37. Christological Significance

Genesis 2:25 contributes to the larger biblical movement from innocence to shame, from alienation to reconciliation, and from nakedness to covering.

The Bible's redemptive story ultimately addresses the shame and alienation introduced by sin.

Christ's saving work provides the foundation for forgiveness, justification, cleansing, and restoration.

---

## 38. Christ and the Removal of Shame

The New Testament presents believers as accepted before God through Christ.

The shame and condemnation associated with sin are addressed through Christ's redemptive work.

Thus the movement from Genesis 2 to the gospel includes the restoration of humanity's relationship with God.

---

## 39. Christ and the Bride

Genesis 2:25 concludes the first marriage account.

The New Testament later develops marriage as an image of Christ and His church.

The original husband-wife relationship therefore contributes to the canonical bridegroom theme.

---

## 40. Christ and the Church's Purity

Paul describes Christ as sanctifying and cleansing His church.

The imagery of purity and presentation resonates with the biblical movement from the original innocence of Genesis toward redeemed holiness.

The connection should be understood canonically rather than as claiming that Genesis 2:25 directly predicts Christ.

---

## 41. From Nakedness to Clothed Salvation

The Bible repeatedly uses clothing imagery in theological contexts.

Human beings attempt to cover themselves after the Fall.

God later provides coverings.

The New Testament speaks of being clothed with Christ and His righteousness.

The progression demonstrates a larger biblical theme of shame, covering, righteousness, and restoration.

---

## 42. Eschatological Restoration

The biblical storyline ultimately moves toward the restoration of God's people.

The shame and alienation introduced by sin do not have the final word.

Through redemption, God's people are brought into fellowship with Him.

The original harmony of Genesis therefore points forward, in a broader canonical sense, toward the consummation of redemption.

---

## 43. Theological and Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:25 establishes the original condition of humanity as innocent, relationally open, physically unashamed, and situated within a good marital relationship.

It also provides the necessary contrast for understanding the Fall, where sin introduces shame, concealment, fear, alienation, and relational disorder.

---

## 44. Implications for the Doctrine of Creation

Creation is good.

The human body is good.

Sexual differentiation is good.

Marriage is good.

The physical world is not inherently evil.

The Fall represents corruption of God's good creation.

---

## 45. Implications for the Doctrine of Sin

Genesis 2:25 helps define sin by contrast.

Before sin, there is no shame.

After sin, shame appears.

Before sin, there is openness.

After sin, concealment appears.

Before sin, there is harmony.

After sin, alienation appears.

---

## 46. Implications for Marriage

Marriage was designed for trust, intimacy, fidelity, and openness.

The husband and wife were to live without fear or shame toward one another.

This establishes an important creational ideal for marital relationship.

---

## 47. Implications for Sexual Ethics

Sexuality is good within God's created order.

Its proper context is the marital union established in Genesis 2.

The verse therefore supports a positive rather than merely negative theology of sexuality.

---

## 48. Implications for Human Dignity

The human body is not inherently shameful.

Human beings possess dignity because they are God's creatures and image-bearers.

The Fall damages human experience but does not erase humanity's created dignity.

---

## 49. Implications for Christian Anthropology

Human beings are embodied, relational, sexual, moral, and spiritual creatures.

Genesis 2:25 contributes to all of these dimensions.

Human identity cannot be separated from God's creational design.

---

## 50. Implications for the Gospel

The gospel addresses the consequences of the Fall.

Where sin produces guilt and shame, Christ provides forgiveness and righteousness.

Where sin produces alienation, Christ provides reconciliation.

Where sin produces death, Christ provides life.

Genesis 2:25 therefore contributes indirectly to understanding why redemption is necessary.

---

## 51. Canonical Significance

Genesis 2:25 is essential for understanding Genesis 3.

Its importance extends further because the themes of shame, covering, nakedness, purity, marriage, and restoration recur throughout Scripture.

The verse establishes the original condition against which later redemptive realities are understood.

---

## 52. Relation to the Whole Biblical Enterprise

The Bible's story can be viewed in terms of creation, Fall, redemption, and consummation.

Genesis 2:25 belongs to the creation stage.

Genesis 3 introduces the Fall.

The rest of Scripture develops God's redemptive response.

The New Testament presents Christ as the center of that redemption.

The final biblical vision points toward complete restoration.

Genesis 2:25 therefore serves as an important baseline for understanding the entire biblical enterprise.

---

## 53. Final Exegetical Reflection

Genesis 2:25 is a remarkably short verse, but it carries enormous theological weight. It concludes the creation account of Genesis 2 by describing humanity before sin entered the world.

The man and his wife are naked.

And they are not ashamed.

The simplicity of the statement is precisely what makes the later reversal so powerful.

There is no shame.

There is no hiding.

There is no fear.

There is no accusation.

There is no alienation.

The man and woman exist together in complete openness.

This is the original condition of human marriage.

The verse must therefore be read in connection with everything that precedes it.

God created the man.

God declared that it was not good for him to be alone.

God brought the animals before him.

None corresponded to him.

God created the woman.

God brought her to the man.

Adam recognized her as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh.

Scripture established the one-flesh relationship.

And now the husband and wife are described as naked and unashamed.

The sequence is deliberate.

The verse demonstrates that marriage, bodily intimacy, and sexuality belong to God's original good creation.

There is nothing inherently sinful about the human body.

There is nothing inherently sinful about sexual differentiation.

There is nothing inherently sinful about marital intimacy.

The problem enters in Genesis 3, when humanity violates God's command.

This distinction is crucial.

The biblical story does not begin with the body as an enemy of the spirit.

It begins with embodied human beings living in God's good creation.

Adam and his wife are naked because they have nothing to hide.

Their physical vulnerability does not create fear.

Their relationship is safe.

Their bodies are not weapons against one another.

They do not objectify each other.

They do not exploit one another.

They simply exist together in the innocence God created.

This makes the next chapter so tragic.

Genesis 3:7 records that after eating the forbidden fruit, their eyes are opened and they realize that they are naked.

They make coverings.

Later they hide from God.

The same bodies that were previously experienced without shame are now associated with vulnerability and exposure.

The bodies themselves have not become evil.

Sin has changed the human relationship to the body.

Sin has also changed the human relationship to God and to one another.

Genesis 2:25 therefore provides a theological baseline for understanding the effects of the Fall.

Before sin, there is openness.

After sin, concealment.

Before sin, there is trust.

After sin, fear.

Before sin, there is harmony.

After sin, accusation and conflict.

Before sin, there is unashamed intimacy.

After sin, there is shame.

The verse consequently contributes to the doctrine of sin by showing what humanity was before the Fall.

Sin is not merely the addition of individual immoral actions.

It introduces corruption into the entire network of human relationships.

It affects humanity's relationship with God, with oneself, with others, and with creation.

Genesis 2:25 is therefore more than a statement about nudity.

It is a statement about the wholeness of creation.

The man and woman are completely exposed yet completely secure.

They do not need to protect themselves from one another.

Their vulnerability is not dangerous because sin has not yet entered.

This provides an important picture of the goodness of marital intimacy.

Marriage was designed for profound openness.

Husband and wife are to know one another.

They are to share life.

They are to become one flesh.

They are to live in covenantal faithfulness.

Genesis 2:25 shows the relational atmosphere in which that union originally existed.

The verse also contributes to the theology of human dignity.

The human body is not shameful simply because it is a body.

Human beings were created embodied.

The body belongs to God's good creation.

The problem is not physicality.

The problem is rebellion.

This is especially important because later biblical theology sometimes uses the language of flesh negatively when discussing sinful human nature.

Genesis 2 reminds us that “flesh” itself is not inherently evil.

God created embodied humanity as good.

Sin corrupts the person; it does not make created embodiment intrinsically evil.

The verse also prepares the reader for the Bible's later themes of covering and clothing.

After the Fall, Adam and Eve attempt to cover themselves.

The biblical story subsequently develops imagery involving covering, cleansing, righteousness, purity, and clothing.

The New Testament ultimately speaks of being clothed with Christ.

These later themes should not be forced directly into Genesis 2:25 as though the verse explicitly predicts them.

Nevertheless, the canonical trajectory begins with this simple contrast between being naked and unashamed and later experiencing shame and the need for covering.

The Christological significance is therefore best understood through the whole biblical story.

Christ comes into a world where humanity is no longer innocent.

Human beings are guilty.

Human beings are ashamed.

Human beings hide.

Human beings are alienated from God.

The gospel addresses these consequences.

Through Christ, sinners receive forgiveness.

Through Christ, they are justified.

Through Christ, they are reconciled to God.

Through Christ, they are cleansed.

Through Christ, they are given a new standing before God.

The redemption accomplished by Christ therefore answers the alienation introduced in Genesis 3.

Genesis 2:25 is also significant for the biblical bride motif.

The verse concludes the first marriage account.

The first husband and wife live together in perfect openness.

Later Scripture develops the imagery of bride and bridegroom to describe God's covenant relationship with His people.

The New Testament presents Christ as the bridegroom and the church as His bride.

The original marriage therefore becomes part of the Bible's larger theological vocabulary.

Genesis 2:25 does not directly predict Christ and the church.

Its immediate subject is Adam and his wife.

But the canonical development of marriage imagery demonstrates that the institution established in Genesis has theological significance beyond itself.

The verse ultimately points us back to the goodness of God's original design.

Humanity was created for fellowship with God.

Man and woman were created for one another.

Marriage was established as a good institution.

The body was good.

Sexuality was good.

Intimacy was good.

Shame was absent.

Sin changed everything.

Yet the Fall does not have the final word.

The biblical story moves toward redemption and restoration.

The God who created humanity in Genesis does not abandon humanity after the Fall.

He pursues His people.

He provides redemption.

He promises restoration.

The New Testament identifies Christ as the one through whom redemption is accomplished.

The final biblical vision therefore points toward the restoration of God's people in the presence of God.

Genesis 2:25 should consequently be read both as a description of original innocence and as a contrast that helps us understand the tragedy of the Fall and the necessity of redemption.

The verse is short, but its theological implications are extensive.

It tells us what humanity was before sin.

It shows us what marriage was before sin.

It shows us what sexuality was before sin.

It shows us what relational vulnerability was before sin.

And it prepares us to understand what sin would destroy.

The man and his wife were naked.

They were completely exposed.

Yet they were not ashamed.

There was no guilt to conceal.

There was no sin to confess.

There was no fear of judgment.

There was no need to hide.

This was humanity in the goodness of God's original creation.

Genesis 2 therefore ends not with conflict but with peace.

The chapter closes with a husband and wife living in complete innocence.

The tragedy of Genesis 3 has not yet occurred.

The Fall has not yet shattered their fellowship.

The world remains under God's good creational order.

Genesis 2:25 is thus the final quiet moment before the storm of Genesis 3.

It gives the reader a picture of what humanity was meant to be before sin entered.

And because the Bible's story ultimately moves toward restoration, this original condition also provides a conceptual backdrop for the hope of redemption.

God's purpose is not finally defeated by the Fall.

The gospel announces that Christ has come to redeem His people and restore fellowship with God.

The biblical story begins with humanity in God's good creation and ultimately moves toward redeemed humanity dwelling with God.

**Genesis 2:25 therefore presents the original condition of humanity as physically unashamed, relationally transparent, morally innocent, and secure within God's good creational order, establishing the contrast necessary for understanding the shame, concealment, fear, alienation, and corruption introduced by the Fall, while contributing to the broader biblical movement from creation and innocence through sin and redemption toward the restoration accomplished in Christ.**
