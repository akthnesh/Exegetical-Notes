# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:20

## Genesis 2:20

Genesis 2:20 records the conclusion of Adam's naming of the animals and, more importantly, the discovery that none of the creatures provides the corresponding helper announced by the LORD God in Genesis 2:18. The verse therefore serves as a crucial bridge between the creation of the animals and the creation of the woman. What initially appears to be a simple statement concerning Adam's naming activity becomes a profound theological statement about human uniqueness, companionship, marriage, vocation, and the purposeful design of God.

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּקְרָא הָאָדָם שֵׁמוֹת לְכָל־הַבְּהֵמָה וּלְעוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם וּלְכֹל חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה וּלְאָדָם לֹא־מָצָא עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ**

## Transliteration

*Vayyiqrāʾ hāʾādām shēmôt ləḵol-habbəhēmāh ûləʿôf haššāmayim ûləḵol ḥayyat haśśādeh; ûləʾādām lōʾ-māṣāʾ ʿēzer kənegdô.*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the man gave names to all the livestock, and to the birds of the heavens, and to every beast of the field; but for the man he did not find a helper corresponding to him.”**

---

## 1. The Verse in Its Immediate Context

Genesis 2:20 follows God's declaration in verse 18: “It is not good that the man should be alone.” God had promised to make a helper corresponding to him. Verse 19 then described God bringing the animals and birds before Adam so that he would name them. Verse 20 brings that activity to its theological conclusion.

Adam names the animals, but none of them answers the need identified in verse 18.

The repeated movement from verse 18 to verse 20 is deliberate. God identifies Adam's need. Adam encounters the animal creation. Adam exercises his authority by naming the creatures. Yet the search produces no corresponding helper.

The verse therefore creates the narrative expectation that God Himself must provide the answer.

---

## 2. “The Man Gave Names”

The subject remains **the man**, or *hāʾādām*.

Adam is acting as the human representative within creation. The naming activity demonstrates that he is not merely another creature among the animals. He has received a particular vocation from God.

Naming requires recognition and distinction. Adam is capable of engaging intelligently with the created order and assigning designations to its creatures.

The verse thus continues the biblical presentation of humanity as uniquely equipped for responsible dominion.

---

## 3. Naming and Dominion

Adam's naming of the creatures is closely related to the dominion mandate of Genesis 1:26–28.

God created humanity in His image and entrusted humanity with dominion over the living creatures. Genesis 2:20 gives a concrete expression of that dominion.

Adam does not merely observe the animals. He names them.

Yet the authority is delegated. Adam does not create the creatures. God created them and brought them to him.

This distinction is essential. Human dominion is real, but it is not independent of divine authority.

---

## 4. “All the Livestock”

The first category mentioned is the livestock or domesticated animals.

The Hebrew term *bəhemah* generally refers to larger animals, particularly beasts associated with domestication or human use.

The inclusion of livestock demonstrates the breadth of Adam's task.

He encounters creatures that will later be associated with agriculture, labor, food, sacrifice, and human society.

Yet even these creatures cannot answer the fundamental relational need of the man.

---

## 5. “The Birds of the Heavens”

Adam also names the birds.

The phrase connects with the creation language of Genesis 1.

The birds occupy the heavens and represent another sphere of the created order.

Adam therefore encounters creatures from different environments and categories.

The repeated categories emphasize comprehensiveness.

The narrative is not suggesting that Adam simply overlooked one obvious animal companion.

The conclusion is comprehensive: no creature among the animals corresponds to him.

---

## 6. “Every Beast of the Field”

The third category is the beasts of the field.

The expression recalls Genesis 2:19.

The repetition emphasizes that Adam has encountered the animal creation presented by God.

The animals are not being portrayed as inadequate because they are defective.

They are inadequate for a specific purpose because they do not share Adam's human nature.

They are creatures over whom Adam has dominion, not creatures who can become his corresponding human partner.

---

## 7. The Repetition Is Theologically Significant

The verse repeats the animal categories from the previous verse.

This repetition slows the narrative.

The reader is made to feel the comprehensiveness of Adam's experience.

Every relevant category has been considered.

And the result is decisive.

There is no suitable helper.

The repetition therefore reinforces the necessity of what God will do next.

---

## 8. “But for the Man”

The phrase **“for the man”** marks a significant contrast.

The animals have been named.

But Adam himself remains without a corresponding partner.

The text shifts attention from the animals back to Adam.

The animals have their names.

Adam still has no counterpart.

The problem announced in verse 18 remains unresolved.

---

## 9. “He Did Not Find”

The Hebrew verb *māṣāʾ* means “to find.”

The expression does not imply that God was uncertain about the outcome.

Rather, the narrative describes the result from Adam's experiential perspective.

Adam examines the creatures.

He names them.

Yet he does not find among them what God said was necessary.

The process demonstrates the truth through experience.

---

## 10. The Importance of “Not”

The Hebrew negative *lōʾ* gives the verse its decisive conclusion.

**He did not find.**

The result is absence.

No animal fulfills the stated purpose.

This negative conclusion is essential because it prepares for the positive provision in the following verses.

God will not simply leave Adam in his condition.

The absence creates the setting for divine provision.

---

## 11. “A Helper”

The Hebrew noun is **ʿēzer**, commonly translated “helper.”

This word should not be understood as describing someone who is inferior or merely subordinate.

The same Hebrew noun is frequently used of God Himself as the helper of His people.

The meaning must therefore be determined by context.

Here, the helper is one who corresponds to Adam and answers his need.

The woman will be neither Adam's servant nor his superior.

She will be his corresponding partner.

---

## 12. “Corresponding to Him”

The phrase **kənegdô** is particularly important.

It conveys the idea of something or someone corresponding to, opposite to, or suitable for another.

The woman will stand before Adam as his counterpart.

She will share his human nature while being distinct from him as female.

This correspondence is fundamental to the biblical theology of marriage.

---

## 13. The Meaning of Complementarity

The phrase “corresponding to him” establishes the idea of complementarity.

Adam needs someone who corresponds to him.

The answer is not another animal.

The answer is another human being.

The woman will possess the same human dignity and nature while being sexually differentiated from the man.

This complementarity will be expressed climactically in Genesis 2:23–24.

---

## 14. Human Uniqueness

Genesis 2:20 strongly reinforces human uniqueness.

Adam has interacted with the animal creation.

He can name it.

He exercises authority over it.

Yet he cannot find within it a counterpart.

The distinction is therefore not simply about intellectual capacity.

It concerns human nature and vocation.

Adam needs a human counterpart because his calling is uniquely human.

---

## 15. Animals Are Not Human Equivalents

The verse establishes an important boundary.

Animals are valuable creatures.

They belong to God's creation.

They are subject to human stewardship.

But they are not human beings.

They cannot fulfill the covenantal and relational role of a human spouse.

The biblical worldview therefore simultaneously recognizes animal value and human uniqueness.

---

## 16. The Failure of the Search Is Not a Failure of Creation

The absence of a suitable helper should not be interpreted as evidence that God's creation is defective.

The animals fulfill their own created purposes.

The problem is not that the animals are badly made.

The problem is that Adam's need is specifically human.

A bird cannot be Adam's wife because it is not human.

A beast cannot be Adam's corresponding human counterpart because it does not share his nature.

The distinction is purposeful.

---

## 17. The Narrative Logic of Verses 18–20

The sequence is remarkably precise.

Verse 18 establishes the problem.

Verse 19 introduces the animals.

Verse 20 records Adam's naming and the absence of a corresponding helper.

Verse 21 introduces God's action.

Verse 22 describes the creation of the woman.

Verse 23 records Adam's joyful recognition.

Verse 24 establishes the one-flesh principle.

The narrative therefore moves from need to search, from search to absence, from absence to divine provision, and from provision to covenantal union.

---

## 18. Adam's Solitude

The word “alone” in verse 18 receives its narrative explanation here.

Adam is not alone in the sense that no other living beings exist.

He is surrounded by animals.

God is present.

Creation is full of life.

Yet Adam remains alone in a uniquely human relational sense.

The verse therefore teaches that human solitude cannot be solved merely by filling one's environment with creatures.

Human beings need human fellowship.

---

## 19. The Difference Between Companionship and Correspondence

Animals may provide companionship.

But companionship is not identical with correspondence.

Genesis 2 is concerned with a partner who shares Adam's human identity and vocation.

The woman will be capable of reciprocal human relationship, covenant, shared vocation, and one-flesh union.

The animal creation cannot provide this.

The distinction is crucial to understanding the passage.

---

## 20. Adam's Need Is Relational

The problem identified in Genesis 2:18 is relational.

Adam needs someone with whom he can share life and vocation.

The solution is therefore not another tool or possession.

God does not give Adam another piece of equipment.

He gives him another person.

This demonstrates the relational dimension of human existence.

---

## 21. Adam Needs a Counterpart, Not Merely an Assistant

The English word “helper” can sometimes be misunderstood.

The Hebrew expression does not suggest that Adam needs someone merely to perform tasks for him.

The phrase *ʿēzer kənegdô* describes a helper who corresponds to him.

The woman will therefore stand alongside Adam in a relationship of genuine human complementarity.

The task-oriented dimension of the relationship is important, but it does not exhaust its meaning.

---

## 22. Equality of Human Dignity

Because the woman corresponds to Adam, she shares his humanity.

Genesis 1 has already established that both male and female are created in God's image.

Genesis 2 develops the relational dimension of that equality.

The woman is not an animal brought into Adam's dominion.

She is human.

She is of Adam's own nature.

She will be recognized as “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.”

---

## 23. Distinction Without Inferiority

Genesis 2 presents distinction without requiring inferiority.

Adam and the woman are distinct.

They have complementary sexual identities.

Yet the woman's creation from Adam will demonstrate her profound unity with him.

The biblical picture is therefore neither sameness nor inequality of worth.

It is unity with distinction.

---

## 24. The Foundation of Marriage

Genesis 2:20 prepares directly for the doctrine of marriage.

Marriage is not introduced merely as a social invention.

It arises from God's creative design.

God observes Adam's condition.

God identifies the need for a corresponding helper.

God creates the woman.

Adam receives her.

Genesis 2:24 then articulates the one-flesh union.

Marriage therefore belongs to the creational order.

---

## 25. The Importance of Divine Initiative

Adam does not create the woman.

He does not discover her through his own search.

God provides her.

This emphasizes divine initiative.

The relationship between man and woman is rooted in God's creative purpose.

The woman is God's provision.

Marriage therefore carries theological significance beyond social arrangement.

---

## 26. The Woman as God's Answer

Genesis 2:20 ends with a problem.

Genesis 2:21–22 supplies the answer.

This structure is intentional.

The narrative wants the reader to understand the woman as God's answer to Adam's condition.

She is not an afterthought.

She is not an accidental addition.

She is the divinely designed counterpart anticipated by verse 18.

---

## 27. The Meaning of “Corresponding”

The phrase *kənegdô* can be understood as describing someone “corresponding to him” or “suitable for him.”

The concept includes correspondence, suitability, and relational compatibility.

The woman will correspond to Adam in human nature and vocation while complementing him as female.

This is why the creation of the woman resolves the problem that the animals could not solve.

---

## 28. The Man and Woman as a Human Pair

The text moves toward the recognition that humanity is fundamentally relational.

God created humanity as male and female.

Adam's experience demonstrates the incompleteness of solitary human existence within the context of God's stated purpose.

The woman does not make Adam more human.

Rather, she makes possible the particular relational and reproductive union through which God's creational mandate can continue.

---

## 29. Creation and Procreation

Genesis 1:28 commands humanity to be fruitful and multiply.

Genesis 2 provides the relational foundation for that command.

Adam requires a corresponding female human partner.

The woman is therefore not merely emotional companionship.

The male-female union is also related to humanity's calling to fill the earth.

Marriage is connected to the continuation and flourishing of human life.

---

## 30. Adam's Vocation and Shared Vocation

Adam's work in Eden includes cultivation and keeping.

The creation of the woman establishes the possibility of shared human vocation.

The woman is not created merely for passive existence.

She is Adam's corresponding partner.

Together, man and woman participate in humanity's God-given calling.

This shared vocation becomes important for understanding biblical marriage.

---

## 31. A Theology of Human Community

Genesis 2:20 contributes to a larger biblical theology of community.

Human beings were not designed for absolute isolation.

The need for relationship is woven into creation.

The woman is the first explicit human companion presented in the narrative.

The passage therefore establishes a foundation for understanding human community as part of God's creational purpose.

---

## 32. The Limits of Technology and Possessions

Although the immediate context concerns animals rather than technology, the principle has broader implications.

Adam's problem cannot be solved simply by acquiring more things.

He already possesses an extraordinary creation.

He has a vocation.

He has God's presence.

Yet the text identifies a relational need.

Material abundance cannot substitute for meaningful human relationship.

---

## 33. The Limits of Animal Companionship

Modern readers may recognize the value of animals as companions.

Genesis does not deny this value.

But the verse establishes a theological limit.

Animals cannot replace human covenantal relationship.

They can be loved and cared for.

They can accompany human beings.

But they do not possess the same human nature or covenantal vocation.

---

## 34. Theological Implication: Human Dignity

Human dignity is reinforced by the contrast.

Adam is surrounded by living creatures but remains distinct from them.

He is the human image-bearer.

His need for a human counterpart confirms the special character of human life.

Human beings therefore possess a dignity that cannot be reduced to usefulness or biological complexity.

---

## 35. Theological Implication: God's Wisdom

The process demonstrates God's wisdom.

God knows what Adam needs.

He reveals the inadequacy of the animals.

He then provides the woman.

The narrative therefore teaches that God's purposes are intentional.

The woman is not a secondary improvisation.

Her creation is part of God's wise design.

---

## 36. Theological Implication: Divine Providence

God's providence operates through the entire episode.

God creates the animals.

God brings them to Adam.

Adam names them.

God allows the absence of a corresponding helper to become evident.

God then creates the woman.

Every stage belongs to God's providential ordering.

---

## 37. Theological Implication: Delegated Authority

Adam's naming authority remains important.

He is exercising dominion even while experiencing relational incompleteness.

Human authority and human need exist simultaneously.

A person may possess authority over creation while still needing other people.

Dominion does not eliminate dependence.

---

## 38. Theological Implication: Mutual Dependence

The passage begins to establish a biblical theology of mutual human dependence.

Adam needs the woman.

The woman will be created from Adam and for relationship with him.

Neither is presented as autonomous.

Human flourishing occurs within relationships ordered by God.

---

## 39. Interpretative Challenge: What Does “Find” Mean?

The expression “did not find” should not be read as though Adam conducted an independent experiment because God lacked knowledge.

The narrative describes the experiential outcome.

Adam encounters the animals and names them.

None proves suitable as his corresponding partner.

The language communicates discovery from the human perspective.

---

## 40. Interpretative Challenge: Does the Verse Teach Female Subordination?

The verse does not itself establish a doctrine of female inferiority.

The woman is called a *helper corresponding to him*.

The Hebrew term *ʿēzer* can refer to God as helper.

The concept therefore does not intrinsically communicate inferiority.

Questions concerning particular roles within marriage and the church must be addressed through the whole biblical canon rather than by assigning an inferior meaning to “helper” here.

---

## 41. Interpretative Challenge: Is Adam Incomplete as an Individual?

The verse should not be interpreted to mean that every unmarried person is defective or incomplete as a human being.

The immediate context concerns Adam's unique creational situation before the woman is created.

The biblical canon later recognizes both marriage and singleness as meaningful callings.

Genesis 2 establishes the creational pattern of marriage without declaring every individual incapable of meaningful life apart from marriage.

---

## 42. Interpretative Challenge: Does “Helper” Mean Servant?

No.

The concept of *ʿēzer* does not mean “servant” in the sense of an inferior household employee.

The helper is one who provides necessary assistance.

The accompanying phrase *kənegdô* specifies correspondence.

The woman is therefore a fitting counterpart.

---

## 43. Interpretative Challenge: Is the Woman Merely a Solution to Adam's Loneliness?

The woman does answer Adam's solitude, but the passage presents more than emotional companionship.

She corresponds to him as a human partner.

The later one-flesh statement connects their union with marriage.

The creation of woman therefore encompasses companionship, complementarity, shared vocation, sexual union, and covenantal relationship.

---

## 44. Christological Significance

Genesis 2:20 contributes to the biblical background for the New Testament's theology of Adam and Christ.

Adam is the first human representative.

His life unfolds within the creational order.

The New Testament identifies Christ as the last Adam and the second man.

The comparison becomes significant because Christ comes as the representative head of a redeemed humanity.

---

## 45. Adam and Christ

Adam's story begins with God's provision in Eden.

The broader story will reveal Adam's failure and humanity's fall.

Christ comes as the obedient representative who accomplishes redemption.

Thus the human story beginning in Genesis 2 eventually becomes part of the theological framework for understanding Christ's saving work.

---

## 46. Christ and the Bride

The creation of the woman from the man has also been connected typologically with the New Testament's presentation of Christ and His church.

The New Testament uses marriage imagery to describe Christ's relationship with His people.

Genesis 2 therefore provides foundational imagery for later biblical teaching concerning covenantal union, sacrificial love, and the relationship between Christ and the church.

The typological development should be distinguished from the immediate meaning of Genesis 2:20, but the canonical connection is significant.

---

## 47. Christ and Human Community

The biblical story ultimately moves toward a redeemed community gathered around Christ.

The solitary man in Eden is the beginning of a human family.

The church is described as a people united in Christ.

The movement from isolation toward covenant community therefore resonates with the larger biblical narrative.

---

## 48. Theological and Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:20 teaches that God intentionally designed human beings for relational existence. It reinforces human uniqueness, delegated dominion, the goodness of animal creation, the insufficiency of animals as human counterparts, the equality of human dignity between male and female, the complementarity of man and woman, and the divine institution of marriage.

It also demonstrates God's providential wisdom. Adam's need is not solved through his own autonomous effort. God reveals the insufficiency of the animal creation and then supplies what Adam requires.

---

## 49. Implications for Marriage

Marriage should be understood as a creational institution.

It is rooted in God's design for male-female correspondence.

It involves companionship, shared vocation, and one-flesh union.

The woman is neither an inferior accessory nor a replacement for Adam's identity.

She is his corresponding human partner.

---

## 50. Implications for Christian Anthropology

Christian anthropology begins with God's creation rather than merely with human self-definition.

Human beings are created by God.

They bear His image.

They possess a unique vocation.

They are relational.

They are male and female.

Their relationships are therefore part of God's creational design.

---

## 51. Implications for Human Society

Human society is not merely a product of biological necessity.

Relationality is part of God's design.

Family, marriage, friendship, community, and fellowship all belong within the broader reality that human beings were not created for absolute isolation.

Genesis 2:20 therefore provides a foundation for thinking about human society in theological terms.

---

## 52. Implications for Work and Vocation

Adam's naming activity shows that human work can be intellectual and administrative.

The woman will become his corresponding partner within the larger human vocation.

Work therefore belongs within the created order.

The Fall will introduce frustration and difficulty, but work itself is not a post-Fall invention.

---

## 53. Implications for the Care of Creation

Adam's dominion remains genuine even though he needs companionship.

The animals are God's creatures.

They are to be named and governed, not despised.

Human beings should therefore exercise authority responsibly.

The verse supports a theology in which creation is neither worshiped nor abused.

---

## 54. Implications for Human Identity

Adam's identity is not determined merely by his relationship to the animals.

He is God's image-bearer.

The woman will likewise share this human dignity.

The relationship between man and woman therefore occurs within a shared identity as creatures made for God.

---

## 55. Canonical Significance

Genesis 2:20 introduces themes that will reverberate through the entire Bible.

The themes of human uniqueness, marriage, companionship, covenant, dominion, stewardship, male-female complementarity, and Adamic representation all receive later development.

The verse should therefore be read not merely as an isolated statement about ancient animals but as part of the foundational anthropology of Scripture.

---

## 56. Relation to the Whole Biblical Enterprise

The Bible begins with God's creation of the world and humanity.

Genesis 2 establishes the creational order.

Genesis 3 records the Fall.

The remainder of Scripture traces God's redemptive response to sin.

The New Testament identifies Christ as the last Adam.

The biblical story eventually culminates in the renewal of creation and the gathering of God's redeemed people.

Genesis 2:20 stands near the beginning of this enterprise and establishes categories that remain important throughout Scripture.

---

## 57. Final Exegetical Reflection

Genesis 2:20 is the decisive negative conclusion to the animal-naming episode. Adam names the livestock, the birds, and the beasts of the field, but despite the abundance of life surrounding him, no creature is found to be a corresponding helper.

The significance of this conclusion becomes clear when it is read alongside Genesis 2:18. God had already declared that Adam's solitary condition was “not good.” The naming episode demonstrates why. Adam's environment is full, but he remains relationally alone in a uniquely human sense.

The animals cannot resolve this condition because they do not correspond to Adam. They are God's creatures, and Adam has authority over them, but they do not share his human nature. They cannot participate with him in the unique covenantal, relational, and vocational union that God intends.

This distinction is fundamental to biblical anthropology. Humanity is part of creation, but humanity is not merely another category of animal life. Human beings bear God's image and have been entrusted with dominion. Adam's naming of the animals demonstrates that delegated authority. Yet the same episode shows the limits of that authority. Dominion over creation cannot replace human fellowship.

The verse also provides a powerful foundation for understanding marriage. The woman who appears in the following verses is not simply another creature. She is God's provision of a corresponding human partner. She shares Adam's humanity while complementing him as female. Her creation answers the problem identified by God Himself.

The Hebrew expression *ʿēzer kənegdô* is especially important. The word *ʿēzer* does not mean an inferior servant. Scripture itself can use the language of “help” for God. The phrase *kənegdô* adds the idea of correspondence and suitability. The woman will therefore be a helper who corresponds to Adam, not someone whose dignity is beneath his.

The verse consequently establishes unity with distinction. The man and woman are not identical, but neither are they different species or unequal in human dignity. They are corresponding human persons. Their distinction is precisely what makes the one-flesh union possible.

The passage also reveals the wisdom of God. Adam does not independently solve his condition. God orchestrates the process. He brings the animals, allows Adam to name them, and allows the absence of a suitable counterpart to become evident. Then God acts.

Theologically, this is a picture of divine providence. God knows what He intends to accomplish, yet He brings Adam through a real experience that prepares him to recognize God's provision. The narrative therefore teaches not divine uncertainty but purposeful revelation.

Genesis 2:20 also has important implications for work. Adam's naming activity demonstrates that human vocation includes intellectual engagement with creation. Human beings are capable of observation, classification, language, and responsible administration. The created world is intelligible, and humanity is equipped to engage it.

At the same time, the verse guards against autonomous humanism. Adam's authority is delegated. He names creatures that God created. He exercises dominion within a world that belongs to God. Human beings are stewards, not ultimate owners.

This has implications for how creation should be treated. Animals should neither be worshiped nor despised. They are God's creatures and therefore possess creaturely value. Humanity's unique status does not justify irresponsible exploitation. Instead, human dominion should reflect the character of the Creator who entrusted it.

The Christological significance emerges within the broader canonical story. Adam is the first representative man. His story will eventually lead to the Fall and the entrance of sin into human history. The New Testament later identifies Jesus Christ as the last Adam. Christ comes as the representative head of redeemed humanity and accomplishes what the first Adam failed to accomplish.

The marriage imagery that begins here also develops throughout Scripture. The New Testament speaks of Christ and the church using the language of bridegroom and bride. While Genesis 2:20 itself concerns Adam's immediate need for a human counterpart, the canonical development allows the creation of the woman and the one-flesh relationship to provide foundational imagery for later covenantal theology.

Genesis 2:20 therefore should not be dismissed as merely a transitional verse. It is a carefully constructed theological hinge. The animals have been named. Adam has exercised dominion. The created world surrounds him. Yet none corresponds to him.

The absence is purposeful.

It prepares for God's provision.

The woman will come not as an afterthought but as the answer to the need announced by God in verse 18. Adam will recognize her as his own kind, his own flesh, his corresponding partner.

The progression is therefore unmistakable: God creates, God orders, Adam exercises delegated authority, Adam discovers the limits of animal companionship, God provides the corresponding human partner, and the man and woman enter the creational relationship that will be defined as one flesh.

Genesis 2:20 teaches that human beings are uniquely created for God, uniquely responsible within creation, and uniquely relational within humanity. It establishes the foundation for understanding marriage not as a human invention but as a divine creational institution.

Above all, the verse teaches that what Adam could not find through his own examination of creation, God Himself would provide. The answer to Adam's solitude comes from God's wise and gracious initiative.

**Genesis 2:20 therefore functions as the decisive preparation for the creation of woman: Adam exercises God-given dominion by naming the animal creation, yet no creature corresponds to him, thereby revealing humanity's unique nature and relational vocation and establishing the necessity of God's provision of the woman as Adam's corresponding human partner.**
