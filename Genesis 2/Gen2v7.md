# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:7

## Genesis 2:7

> **“then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature.”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּיצֶר יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאָדָם עָפָר מִן־הָאֲדָמָה וַיִּפַּח בְּאַפָּיו נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים וַיְהִי הָאָדָם לְנֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה**

## Transliteration

*Wayyiṣer YHWH ʾĕlōhîm ʾeṯ-hāʾādām ʿāfār min-hāʾăḏāmāh wayyippaḥ bəʾappāyw nišmaṯ ḥayyîm wayəhî hāʾādām lənefeš ḥayyāh*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the LORD God formed the man, dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature.”**

---

## 1. “Then the LORD God formed the man”

Genesis 2:7 is one of the most significant verses in the biblical doctrine of humanity.

The verse begins:

**“Then the LORD God formed the man…”**

The Hebrew verb is:

**וַיִּיצֶר (*wayyiṣer*)**

from the root:

**יָצַר (*yāṣar*)**

meaning:

**“to form,” “to fashion,” “to shape,” or “to mold.”**

The imagery is that of a craftsman forming something deliberately and purposefully.

Genesis 1 presented humanity as the climax of God's creation, made in His image.

Genesis 2 now brings the reader close to the formation of the man.

The God who created humanity in Genesis 1 is here portrayed as the divine Craftsman who personally forms Adam.

The verse therefore emphasizes both God's sovereignty and His intimate involvement in human creation.

---

## 2. The significance of “the LORD God”

The divine name used throughout this section is:

**יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים (*YHWH Elohim*)**

This combines the covenant name **YHWH** with **Elohim**, the name prominent in Genesis 1.

The combination is theologically rich.

The God who created the heavens and earth is the same LORD who personally forms the man.

There is no separation between the God of creation and the God who enters into covenant relationship with humanity.

The Creator is personal.

He is not an impersonal force.

He forms Adam deliberately and gives him life.

The verse therefore establishes a foundational biblical anthropology: human existence originates in the personal creative action of God.

---

## 3. “The man” — הָאָדָם (*hāʾādām*)

The Hebrew expression is:

**הָאָדָם (*hāʾādām*)**

meaning:

**“the man.”**

The definite article is significant.

The narrative is now introducing the human being who will occupy the central role in the events that follow.

The word **אָדָם (*ʾādām*)** can function as a personal name, “Adam,” but it is also related to the general term for humanity or man.

This creates an important wordplay with:

**אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*) — ground.**

Adam is the man formed from the ground.

The relationship between the man and the ground is therefore embedded in the language of the text.

---

## 4. “Dust from the ground”

Genesis 2:7 says that God formed the man from:

**“dust from the ground.”**

The Hebrew word for dust is:

**עָפָר (*ʿāfār*)**

and the word for ground is:

**אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*).**

The text emphasizes human creatureliness.

Human beings are not self-originating.

Adam does not emerge from himself.

God forms him from material that God Himself created.

Humanity therefore possesses genuine dignity, but humanity is not divine.

The biblical view of humanity holds together two truths that must never be separated:

**human beings are creatures of the dust,**

and

**human beings are made by God for a uniquely exalted vocation.**

Genesis 2:7 establishes the first truth while Genesis 1:26–28 establishes the second.

---

## 5. Dust and human mortality

The reference to dust also anticipates an important biblical theme: human mortality.

Genesis 3:19 will later declare:

**“for you are dust, and to dust you shall return.”**

The same ground from which Adam is formed will receive Adam's body after death.

Genesis 2:7 therefore contains, in seed form, the biblical teaching concerning the frailty and mortality of fallen humanity.

The human body is not eternal in itself.

Life is received from God.

When God withdraws the gift of life, the body returns to the earth from which it came.

This distinction between creaturely dependence and divine life-giving power becomes central to biblical anthropology.

---

## 6. “And breathed into his nostrils”

The verse continues:

**“and breathed into his nostrils…”**

The Hebrew verb is:

**נָפַח (*nāpaḥ*)**

meaning:

**“to blow,” “to breathe,” or “to breathe upon.”**

The image is intensely personal.

The LORD God forms the man from dust and then breathes into his nostrils.

The narrative therefore moves from the physical formation of the body to the divine impartation of life.

The text does not present the human body as alive merely because its physical components have been assembled.

Life comes from God.

This is one of the fundamental claims of biblical anthropology.

---

## 7. “The breath of life”

The Hebrew phrase is:

**נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים (*nišmaṯ ḥayyîm*)**

meaning:

**“the breath of life.”**

The noun **נְשָׁמָה (*nəšāmāh*)** is associated with breath or life-breath.

The noun **חַיִּים (*ḥayyîm*)** means:

**“life.”**

The combination emphasizes the divine source of Adam's living existence.

God is the giver of life.

The man receives life rather than possessing independent life within himself.

This becomes a recurring biblical theme.

God gives breath.

God sustains life.

God can take life.

God alone possesses life in an ultimate and underived sense.

---

## 8. “And the man became a living creature”

The final clause says:

**“and the man became a living creature.”**

The Hebrew expression is:

**לְנֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה (*lənefeš ḥayyāh*)**

literally:

**“a living soul/being.”**

The noun **נֶפֶשׁ (*nefeš*)** has a broad semantic range.

Depending upon context, it may refer to:

**life, living being, person, self, or soul.**

Here it describes the man as a living being.

This does not necessarily mean that God placed an immaterial “soul” into an otherwise complete body as though the two were independently existing substances.

Rather, the Hebrew describes the whole man becoming a living being through God's life-giving action.

---

## 9. Body and life

Genesis 2:7 provides an important biblical framework for understanding human beings.

The text presents:

**dust + divine breath → living man.**

The body is real.

The breath of life is real.

The resulting person is a living creature.

Human beings are therefore embodied creatures.

Biblical anthropology does not treat the physical body as an accidental shell.

The body belongs to God's good creation.

Human life is embodied life.

This becomes important later when biblical redemption culminates not in the permanent escape of the soul from the body but in resurrection.

---

## 10. Genesis 2:7 and Genesis 1:26–28

Genesis 1:26–28 tells us that humanity is created in God's image and given dominion over creation.

Genesis 2:7 explains more closely how the man enters that created order.

The two accounts complement each other.

Genesis 1 emphasizes:

**image,**

**likeness,**

**male and female,**

**dominion,**

and

**fruitfulness.**

Genesis 2 emphasizes:

**formation,**

**dust,**

**divine breath,**

**living existence,**

and eventually

**vocation.**

Together they provide a rich theology of humanity.

Human beings are both exalted and humble.

They are image-bearers, yet creatures of dust.

They exercise dominion, yet depend entirely upon God.

---

## 11. Human dignity and human humility

Genesis 2:7 prevents two opposite errors in thinking about humanity.

The first error is to reduce humanity to mere material existence.

The second is to exalt humanity into something divine.

The biblical position is more balanced.

Human beings are formed from dust.

Yet God Himself gives them life.

Genesis 1 adds that humanity bears God's image.

Therefore humanity possesses extraordinary dignity without possessing deity.

This provides the foundation for a biblical understanding of human worth.

Human value is not based upon human autonomy.

It is based upon God's creative purpose.

---

## 12. The wordplay between Adam and adamah

One of the most important literary features of the verse is the relationship between:

**אָדָם (*ʾādām*) — man**

and

**אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*) — ground.**

The man comes from the ground.

The man will work the ground.

The man will eventually return to the ground.

Genesis therefore presents Adam's earthly identity through deliberate wordplay.

Human beings are deeply connected to the created earth.

But humanity's origin from the ground does not mean humanity is merely earthly.

God gives Adam life.

God gives Adam His image-bearing vocation.

The creature formed from dust becomes the creature commissioned to represent God's rule within creation.

---

## 13. The divine breath and creaturely dependence

Adam's first moment of life is an act of dependence.

He does not breathe himself into existence.

He does not create his own life.

God gives him the breath of life.

This makes dependence part of humanity's original condition.

Sin later manifests itself partly through humanity's desire for autonomy.

Genesis 2:7 establishes the opposite truth:

**life is received from God.**

The human being exists because God gives existence.

The human being continues because God sustains life.

Therefore the proper posture of humanity is not autonomous self-rule but grateful dependence upon the Creator.

---

## 14. The breath of God elsewhere in Scripture

The biblical association between divine breath and life continues beyond Genesis.

Job speaks of God's Spirit and breath as sustaining life.

The Psalms describe God as the giver and taker of breath.

Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones uses breath as an image of God's power to restore life.

The New Testament also uses life-giving imagery in connection with the Spirit.

These later passages should not be read as claiming that Genesis 2:7 directly teaches every later doctrine concerning the Holy Spirit.

Rather, they demonstrate how the biblical canon develops the foundational theme introduced here:

**life ultimately comes from God.**

---

## 15. Genesis 2:7 and the image of God

Genesis 2:7 does not repeat the phrase **“image of God.”**

That statement occurs in Genesis 1:26–27.

Nevertheless, Genesis 2:7 describes the same humanity.

The man formed from dust is the man who bears God's image.

This is crucial.

Human dignity does not come from being non-material.

It comes from being God's creature and image-bearer.

The material body is part of God's good design.

Therefore biblical Christianity rejects both materialistic reductionism and contempt for the physical body.

The body is God's creation.

The human person bears God's image.

---

## 16. Genesis 2:7 and the doctrine of creation

Genesis 2:7 strongly supports the doctrine of creation.

Human beings do not arise as autonomous products of chance within the theological framework of Genesis.

The LORD God forms the man.

The LORD God gives him life.

The LORD God establishes his vocation.

The entire existence of humanity is therefore grounded in God's creative will.

This provides the foundation for later biblical teaching concerning human accountability.

The creature belongs to the Creator.

Therefore the creature is responsible to the Creator.

---

## 17. Genesis 2:7 and human accountability

The fact that God personally creates Adam establishes the context for God's commands.

Adam is not an independent sovereign.

He is God's creature.

The One who gives Adam life also gives Adam boundaries.

This becomes immediately relevant in Genesis 2:16–17.

God permits Adam to eat from the trees of the garden but forbids eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

The command is meaningful because Adam belongs to God.

The Creator has authority over His creature.

Thus Genesis 2:7 lays the anthropological foundation for the moral relationship developed in the following verses.

---

## 18. Genesis 2:7 and vocation

Genesis 2:7 should also be connected with verses 5 and 15.

Verse 5 introduces the absence of a man to work the ground.

Verse 7 introduces the man.

Verse 15 gives him the task of working and keeping the garden.

This creates a deliberate narrative progression:

**No man to work the ground → God forms the man → God places the man in the garden → God gives him work.**

Human vocation is therefore not an afterthought.

It is part of the Creator's design.

Adam is formed for relationship with God and for faithful service within creation.

---

## 19. Work is not the curse

Genesis 2:7 contributes indirectly to a crucial theology of work.

Adam is formed before the Fall.

He is then given meaningful work.

Therefore labor itself cannot be identified with the curse.

Genesis 3 will introduce painful toil, frustration, and resistance from the ground.

The curse affects work.

It does not invent the concept of work.

This distinction matters for biblical theology.

Human labor is originally good.

The problem is not that humans work.

The problem is that sin makes work painful, frustrating, and often oppressive.

---

## 20. Genesis 2:7 and the Fall

The contrast between Genesis 2:7 and Genesis 3 is profound.

In Genesis 2:

**God gives life.**

**God provides the garden.**

**God gives vocation.**

**God establishes fellowship.**

In Genesis 3:

**sin enters.**

**alienation follows.**

**the ground is cursed.**

**death becomes the human destiny.**

The declaration:

**“you are dust, and to dust you shall return”**

directly echoes Genesis 2:7.

The same ground from which Adam was formed becomes the place to which his body returns.

Thus Genesis 2:7 provides the foundation for understanding both human life and human death.

---

## 21. Genesis 2:7 and death

Death is not merely the biological cessation of life in Genesis.

It is connected to humanity's rebellion against God.

Genesis 2:7 establishes life as God's gift.

Genesis 2:17 establishes death as the consequence of disobedience.

Genesis 3 narrates the entrance of death into human experience.

Thus biblical anthropology must be understood covenantally.

Human beings live under God's authority.

Life is God's gift.

Disobedience brings death.

Redemption must therefore address the problem of sin and death.

---

## 22. Christ and the first Adam

Genesis 2:7 becomes especially important when read alongside the New Testament's teaching concerning Adam and Christ.

Paul repeatedly contrasts Adam and Christ.

Adam represents humanity under sin and death.

Christ represents the redeemed humanity brought into righteousness and life.

Romans 5 develops the contrast between Adam's trespass and Christ's obedience.

1 Corinthians 15 develops the contrast between Adam and Christ in relation to death and resurrection.

The first Adam is formed from the dust.

Christ, the last Adam, brings resurrection life.

Thus Genesis 2:7 provides the foundation for one of the New Testament's most significant Christological patterns.

---

## 23. “The first man Adam became a living being”

1 Corinthians 15:45 explicitly reflects the language of Genesis 2:7.

Paul writes that:

**“The first man Adam became a living being.”**

He then contrasts Adam with Christ, who is described as the life-giving Spirit.

Paul's point is not that Christ ceases to be human or that the physical creation is evil.

His argument concerns the distinction between natural human life and resurrection life.

Adam receives life.

Christ gives resurrection life.

Adam belongs to the earthly order.

Christ is the risen Lord who inaugurates the heavenly and resurrection order.

Genesis 2:7 therefore becomes part of Paul's theology of resurrection.

---

## 24. Christ as the Last Adam

The title **“last Adam”** in 1 Corinthians 15:45 is deeply significant.

The first Adam receives life from God.

The last Adam, Christ, becomes the source of resurrection life for His people.

The first Adam is associated with the entrance of death.

Christ is associated with the defeat of death.

The first Adam is formed from dust.

Christ rises bodily from the dead and becomes the beginning of the resurrection harvest.

The contrast demonstrates the unity of biblical redemptive history.

Genesis is not merely the beginning of an isolated story.

It establishes patterns that reach their fulfillment in Christ.

---

## 25. Genesis 2:7 and the incarnation

The incarnation provides another profound connection.

The eternal Son truly became man.

The Christian confession does not teach that Christ merely appeared human.

He assumed genuine human nature.

The Son entered the created order as a true human being.

This means that the material human body is not beneath God's concern.

The same biblical theology that begins with God forming man from dust reaches its astonishing climax when the Son takes on human flesh.

The incarnation therefore affirms the goodness and significance of embodied human existence.

---

## 26. Genesis 2:7 and resurrection

Genesis 2:7 also prepares for the biblical doctrine of bodily resurrection.

If humanity were essentially an immaterial soul trapped inside an evil body, resurrection would be unnecessary.

But Genesis presents embodied humanity as God's good creation.

The New Testament therefore looks forward to the resurrection of the body.

1 Corinthians 15 argues that the resurrection body is transformed, imperishable, and suited to the age to come.

The gospel does not ultimately rescue humanity from embodiment.

It redeems embodied humanity.

Genesis 2:7 is part of the foundation for that biblical anthropology.

---

## 27. Genesis 2:7 and the whole biblical enterprise

Genesis 2:7 is foundational for several major biblical themes:

**creation,**

**humanity,**

**image-bearing,**

**vocation,**

**sin,**

**death,**

**redemption,**

**incarnation,**

**resurrection,**

and

**new creation.**

The verse begins with dust and divine breath.

The Bible's larger story eventually moves through death and resurrection toward the renewal of creation.

Thus Genesis 2:7 belongs to the opening movement of the entire biblical enterprise.

---

## 28. Literary function within Genesis 2

Genesis 2:7 is the central transition from the description of the ground to the introduction of humanity.

Verse 5 says there was no man.

Verse 6 describes the watering of the ground.

Verse 7 introduces the man.

Verse 8 introduces Eden.

Verse 15 gives Adam his vocation.

Verse 16 gives Adam God's command.

Verse 18 introduces the need for a corresponding helper.

The narrative therefore builds from:

**environment → man → garden → vocation → command → relationship.**

Genesis 2:7 is the decisive human-centered transition.

---

## 29. The central theological proposition

Genesis 2:7 can be summarized in this central proposition:

**The LORD God personally formed humanity from the ground and gave the man life by His own life-giving breath, establishing humanity as a dependent yet dignified creature created for fellowship with God and faithful vocation within creation.**

Or more simply:

**Human life is God's gift, and humanity's dignity and vocation are grounded in the Creator who formed and enlivened the man.**

---

## Exegetical Outline

**Genesis 2:7**

**“The LORD God formed the man”**

→ God is the personal Creator of humanity.

→ Human existence originates in divine intention.

**“Dust from the ground”**

→ Humanity is genuinely creaturely.

→ Adam's name is connected with the ground.

→ Human mortality is anticipated.

**“Breathed into his nostrils”**

→ God personally gives life.

→ Human existence is dependent upon divine action.

**“The breath of life”**

→ Life is received from God.

→ God is the ultimate source and sustainer of human life.

**“The man became a living creature”**

→ The result is a complete living human being.

→ Biblical anthropology is embodied rather than merely spiritual.

**Central idea:**

**Genesis 2:7 teaches that God personally forms humanity from the earth and gives life to the man, establishing both human creatureliness and human dignity while preparing the biblical foundations for vocation, covenant responsibility, death, redemption, and resurrection.**

---

## Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:7 establishes or reinforces these major doctrines:

1. **Creation** — humanity is deliberately created by God.
2. **Divine sovereignty** — God determines the origin and purpose of human life.
3. **Human creatureliness** — humanity comes from the dust of the ground.
4. **Human dignity** — humanity is personally fashioned by God and, according to Genesis 1, bears His image.
5. **Human dependence** — life is received from God.
6. **Embodied humanity** — the human person is not merely an immaterial soul.
7. **Human mortality** — humanity's connection with dust anticipates return to the ground.
8. **Human vocation** — the man is created to serve God within creation.
9. **Moral accountability** — the Creator has rightful authority over His creature.
10. **The goodness of the body** — physical existence belongs to God's good creation.
11. **The Fall** — sin brings death and corrupts the human vocation.
12. **Adam's federal significance** — Adam becomes the representative head whose sin affects humanity.
13. **Christ as the Last Adam** — Christ fulfills and surpasses Adam's role.
14. **Incarnation** — Christ truly enters embodied human existence.
15. **Resurrection** — redemption culminates in transformed embodied life.
16. **New creation** — the biblical story moves from original creation toward final restoration.

---

## A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

Genesis 2:7 can be structured around three propositions:

### I. GOD FORMS HUMANITY

**“The LORD God formed the man…”**

Human existence is intentional.

### II. GOD GIVES HUMANITY LIFE

**“…and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life…”**

Human life is received from God and remains dependent upon Him.

### III. GOD GIVES HUMANITY PURPOSE

**“…and the man became a living creature.”**

The living man will enter God's garden, receive God's command, and exercise God's appointed vocation.

**Main proposition:**

**Genesis 2:7 teaches that humanity owes both its existence and its life to God, possessing profound dignity as God's creature while remaining completely dependent upon the Creator.**

The canonical movement can be summarized:

**Genesis 1:26–28**  
Humanity is created in God's image and given dominion.

**Genesis 2:5–7**  
God forms Adam from the ground and gives him life.

**Genesis 2:15–17**  
God gives Adam vocation and covenant command.

**Genesis 3:17–19**  
Sin brings curse and death.

**Romans 5:12–21**  
Adam's trespass brings condemnation and death; Christ's obedience brings justification and life.

**1 Corinthians 15:21–22**  
Death comes through Adam; resurrection comes through Christ.

**1 Corinthians 15:45–49**  
The first Adam is contrasted with Christ, the last Adam.

**2 Corinthians 5:17**  
Those united to Christ become new creation.

**Revelation 21:1–5**  
God brings His redemptive purposes to consummation in the renewed creation.

Genesis 2:7 therefore stands as one of Scripture's foundational statements about humanity.

The man is formed from dust, but he is not merely dust.

He becomes a living creature because God gives him life.

His existence is therefore dependent upon the Creator, his dignity is grounded in God's creative purpose, and his vocation is defined by the God who formed him.

The story that begins with Adam's creation will eventually reveal Adam's fall, humanity's descent into sin and death, and God's gracious provision of redemption through the Last Adam, Jesus Christ.

**The God who formed man from the dust is the God who gives life, sustains His creatures, judges sin, and ultimately restores life through Christ.**
