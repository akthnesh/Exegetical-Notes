# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:23

## Genesis 2:23

> **“Then the man said, ‘This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.’”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וַיֹּאמֶר הָאָדָם זֹאת הַפַּעַם עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי וּבָשָׂר מִבְּשָׂרִי לְזֹאת יִקָּרֵא אִשָּׁה כִּי מֵאִישׁ לֻקֳחָה־זֹּאת**

## Transliteration

*Vayyōmer hāʾādām: zōʾt happaʿam, ʿeṣem mēʿăṣāmay ûbāśār mibbəśārî; ləzōʾt yiqqārēʾ ʾiššāh, kî mēʾîš luqqăḥāh-zōʾt.*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the man said, ‘This time, bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; this one shall be called Woman, because from Man this one was taken.’”**

---

## 1. The Verse in Its Immediate Context

Genesis 2:23 records Adam's first recorded words after God brings the woman to him. The preceding verses explain that no creature among the animals was found to be a corresponding helper for Adam. God therefore caused Adam to sleep, took from him, built the woman, and brought her to him.

Adam now responds.

His response is poetry, recognition, delight, and theological confession. He recognizes that the woman is fundamentally different from the animals because she corresponds to him in a way that no other creature does. She shares his human nature and comes from him.

The verse therefore provides the human response to the divine creation described in verses 21–22 and prepares directly for the one-flesh principle of verse 24.

---

## 2. “Then the Man Said”

The Hebrew text uses **הָאָדָם (*hāʾādām*)**, “the man.”

Adam now speaks.

This is the first recorded human poetic declaration in Scripture.

The first words attributed to Adam concern the woman God has brought to him.

That placement is significant. The biblical narrative moves from God's creative action to human recognition and response.

---

## 3. Adam's First Recorded Words

Adam's first recorded speech is not a complaint.

He does not question God's provision.

He does not respond with hesitation.

He responds with joy and recognition.

His words communicate astonishment and delight at discovering the counterpart God has provided.

---

## 4. “This at Last”

The ESV translates the expression **זֹאת הַפַּעַם (*zōʾt happaʿam*)** as “This at last.”

The phrase can carry the sense of “this time” or “now this one.”

It stands in contrast with the preceding search among the animals.

The animals had been brought before Adam, but none corresponded to him.

Now, finally, the appropriate counterpart has appeared.

---

## 5. The Significance of “At Last”

Adam's words express recognition after an unsuccessful search.

The animals could not provide what he needed.

Now God presents the woman.

Adam immediately recognizes the difference.

The expression therefore marks the climax of the preceding narrative.

---

## 6. “Bone of My Bones”

Adam declares:

**“bone of my bones.”**

The Hebrew word **עֶצֶם (*ʿeṣem*)** means “bone.”

The plural possessive expression indicates that the woman belongs intimately to Adam's own humanity.

This is not merely a statement about physical composition.

It is a declaration of kinship, unity, and correspondence.

---

## 7. “Flesh of My Flesh”

Adam continues:

**“and flesh of my flesh.”**

The Hebrew word **בָּשָׂר (*bāśār*)** means “flesh.”

The parallel expressions “bone of my bones” and “flesh of my flesh” intensify the declaration.

Adam recognizes the woman as belonging to his own human nature.

---

## 8. Bone and Flesh as Kinship Language

In Scripture, “bone and flesh” language can express close familial relationship.

It communicates common identity and belonging.

Adam therefore recognizes the woman as his own kind.

She is not merely another creature.

She is human like him.

---

## 9. The Woman's Human Nature

This is one of the most important theological implications of the verse.

Adam recognizes that the woman is genuinely human.

She shares his nature.

The animals could not satisfy the requirement of correspondence because they were not his human counterpart.

The woman is.

---

## 10. The Difference Between Woman and Animals

The preceding verses create a deliberate contrast.

The animals were brought to Adam.

Adam named them.

But none was found to correspond to him.

The woman is different.

Adam immediately recognizes her as “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.”

The text therefore sharply distinguishes human beings from the animal creation.

---

## 11. The Woman as Corresponding Partner

The statement confirms the purpose announced in Genesis 2:18.

God promised a helper corresponding to Adam.

The woman is the answer.

Adam's poetic declaration demonstrates that he recognizes the correspondence.

She is his counterpart.

---

## 12. “She Shall Be Called”

Adam then says:

**“she shall be called Woman.”**

The Hebrew verb **קָרָא (*qārāʾ*)** means “to call” or “to name.”

Naming in Genesis can communicate recognition, identification, and authority.

Adam gives the woman a name corresponding to his own.

---

## 13. Woman and Man

The Hebrew word for “woman” is **אִשָּׁה (*ʾiššāh*)**.

The Hebrew word for “man” in this statement is **אִישׁ (*ʾîš*)**.

The relationship between these words creates a deliberate wordplay.

Adam identifies the woman in a way that highlights her correspondence to himself.

---

## 14. Linguistic Correspondence

The relationship between *ʾîš* and *ʾiššāh* expresses more than sound similarity.

It contributes to the theological point.

The man and woman belong together as corresponding members of humanity.

Their names reflect both unity and distinction.

---

## 15. “Because She Was Taken Out of Man”

Adam explains the naming.

The woman is called *ʾiššāh* because she was taken from *ʾîš*.

The statement connects the woman's identity with her origin.

She came from the man.

This directly recalls the action of God in verses 21–22.

---

## 16. Adam Understands the Divine Work

Adam was asleep while God performed the act described in verse 21.

Yet after awakening and seeing the woman, he recognizes the significance of what God has done.

God has given him a human counterpart from his own flesh.

Adam's statement therefore interprets the divine action.

---

## 17. Recognition Rather Than Discovery

Adam does not discover the woman through experimentation.

God brings her.

Adam recognizes her.

The difference is important.

The preceding animal procession demonstrated what Adam lacked.

God's presentation of the woman reveals what Adam needed.

---

## 18. The First Human Poem

Genesis 2:23 is commonly recognized as poetic speech.

The balanced phrases—

**“bone of my bones”**

and

**“flesh of my flesh”**

—form a parallel structure.

The statement is emotionally elevated and theologically rich.

Adam responds to God's work with poetic praise.

---

## 19. Poetry as Theological Interpretation

Adam's poetry is not merely romantic language.

It interprets reality.

He identifies the woman's origin, nature, and relationship to himself.

The poem therefore has theological content.

---

## 20. Joy in God's Provision

Adam's response reveals joy.

The words “This at last” express satisfaction.

The man who was previously alone now has a corresponding human companion.

God's provision produces delight.

---

## 21. The Absence of Shame

Genesis 2:25 will state that the man and woman were naked and were not ashamed.

Genesis 2:23 already begins to reveal the purity of the relationship.

Adam sees the woman as his corresponding human partner without the distortion that will characterize human relationships after the Fall.

---

## 22. Marriage and Recognition

Adam's declaration prepares for marriage.

He recognizes the woman as uniquely corresponding to him.

Genesis 2:24 will then explain that a man leaves father and mother and holds fast to his wife.

Recognition precedes union.

The woman is identified as the appropriate partner before the marriage principle is stated.

---

## 23. The Foundation of One-Flesh Union

The phrase “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh” anticipates the statement that the man and woman will become “one flesh.”

The woman comes from Adam.

The two will be united.

The narrative therefore moves from shared origin to marital union.

---

## 24. Unity Without Erasing Distinction

Adam does not say that the woman is identical to him.

He says that she is of his bones and flesh.

She remains “Woman.”

He remains “Man.”

The two are distinct persons.

Their unity does not erase their distinction.

---

## 25. Equality of Human Dignity

Adam's declaration supports the equal humanity of man and woman.

She is his own kind.

She shares his human nature.

Genesis 1:27 has already established male and female as image-bearers.

Genesis 2:23 gives narrative expression to their shared humanity.

---

## 26. Complementarity

The woman is not merely another human being.

She is Adam's corresponding female counterpart.

The linguistic relationship between *ʾîš* and *ʾiššāh* highlights this correspondence.

The verse therefore contributes to the biblical concept of male-female complementarity.

---

## 27. The Woman's Identity

The woman's identity is relationally expressed.

She is “Woman” in relation to “Man.”

This does not mean that she lacks individual personhood.

Rather, the narrative highlights the profound relationship between male and female within God's created order.

---

## 28. Adam's Naming and Stewardship

Adam has already named the animals.

Now he identifies the woman.

The act of naming demonstrates Adam's role within creation.

However, the woman is not placed in the same category as the animals.

His statement about her immediately moves beyond ordinary animal classification into personal recognition and marital relationship.

---

## 29. Naming Does Not Mean Ownership

The fact that Adam calls the woman “Woman” should not be interpreted simply as ownership.

The context emphasizes correspondence and shared humanity.

The woman's name reflects her relationship to Adam.

The passage must therefore be read in light of Genesis 1's affirmation of both male and female as God's image-bearers.

---

## 30. The Meaning of “Woman”

The name *ʾiššāh* identifies the female human in relation to the male human.

The wordplay reinforces their shared identity.

The woman is “woman” because she corresponds to “man.”

The distinction is relational and creational.

---

## 31. Theological Implication: God's Provision Is Recognized

God provides.

Adam recognizes.

This pattern is important.

God's work precedes human understanding.

Adam's response is an act of recognition and gratitude.

---

## 32. Theological Implication: Marriage Is Creational

The statement occurs before the Fall.

The relationship between man and woman therefore belongs to the original good creation.

Marriage is not created by sin.

It is established within God's original purpose.

---

## 33. Theological Implication: Human Beings Are Relational

Adam's response confirms that humanity is designed for meaningful relationship.

The woman is the answer to the solitude declared “not good.”

Human fellowship therefore belongs to the creational order.

---

## 34. Theological Implication: Male and Female Share One Humanity

“Bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh” expresses profound unity.

The man and woman are two persons sharing one human nature.

This becomes a foundation for the biblical understanding of human dignity.

---

## 35. Theological Implication: Sexual Difference Is Meaningful

The terms “man” and “woman” are deliberately distinguished.

Their difference is not erased.

Yet their names are linguistically connected.

The passage therefore presents sexual differentiation within profound human unity.

---

## 36. Theological Implication: God's Creation Produces Joy

Adam responds to God's work with delight.

The woman is not presented as an unwanted burden.

She is received with celebration.

The passage therefore portrays companionship and marriage as good gifts of God.

---

## 37. Theological Implication: Human Recognition of Divine Wisdom

Adam recognizes that God's provision is exactly suited to his need.

The animals were inadequate.

The woman corresponds to him.

The narrative portrays God's wisdom in designing humanity as male and female.

---

## 38. Interpretative Challenge: What Does “This at Last” Mean?

The expression may be understood as “this time” or “now this one.”

The context indicates contrast with the animals.

Adam has encountered many creatures, but none corresponded to him.

The woman is finally the appropriate counterpart.

---

## 39. Interpretative Challenge: Is “Bone and Flesh” Literal?

Adam's statement refers to the woman's physical origin, but it is also relational language.

The phrase expresses common humanity, kinship, and intimate unity.

It should not be reduced to a purely anatomical statement.

---

## 40. Interpretative Challenge: Does Adam's Naming Imply Authority?

Naming does have significance in Genesis.

Yet the immediate context emphasizes recognition and correspondence rather than domination.

The verse should therefore not be used to imply that the woman possesses inferior human status.

---

## 41. Interpretative Challenge: Is Woman Defined Only by Man?

The verse highlights the relationship between man and woman because it concerns their creation and future union.

This does not mean that woman has no identity apart from marriage.

Genesis 1 establishes her independent status as an image-bearing human creature.

The focus here is specifically relational.

---

## 42. Interpretative Challenge: Is This Romantic Poetry?

It certainly contains emotional and poetic elements.

But its significance is greater than romance.

Adam is making a theological statement about the woman's humanity, origin, and correspondence to him.

---

## 43. Christological Significance

Genesis 2:23 contributes to the biblical foundation for the bride and bridegroom motif.

Adam recognizes the woman as uniquely corresponding to him.

The New Testament later describes Christ and His church using marriage imagery.

The first human marriage therefore becomes part of a broader canonical pattern.

---

## 44. Adam and Christ

Adam is the first man in the biblical narrative.

Christ is later identified as the last Adam.

Adam's relationship with the woman belongs to the original creation.

Christ's relationship with His church belongs to redemption.

The comparison helps illuminate the biblical movement from creation to new creation.

---

## 45. Christ and His Bride

Ephesians 5 explicitly draws upon Genesis 2:24.

Paul presents the husband-wife relationship as a mystery pointing toward Christ and the church.

Genesis 2:23 contributes to the foundational imagery by portraying the woman as intimately corresponding to the man.

---

## 46. “Bone and Flesh” and Covenant Unity

The language of bone and flesh provides a powerful expression of belonging.

In marriage, husband and wife become one flesh.

In Christ, believers are united to Him and to one another as His body.

The exact expressions should not be equated, but the biblical themes of belonging and profound union are closely related.

---

## 47. The Bride Theme Across Scripture

The biblical storyline develops marriage imagery progressively.

Genesis presents the first man and woman.

The prophets use marriage imagery for God's covenant relationship with Israel.

The New Testament identifies Christ as bridegroom.

The church is portrayed as His bride.

The final biblical vision culminates in the marriage supper of the Lamb.

Genesis 2:23 therefore participates in a major canonical theme.

---

## 48. Theological and Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:23 establishes the profound unity of man and woman, their shared humanity, their sexual distinction, and the relational foundation of marriage.

Adam's words also demonstrate that the woman is God's suitable provision and that marriage rests upon a creational relationship rather than merely a social convention.

---

## 49. Implications for the Doctrine of Humanity

Humanity is created as male and female.

Adam's words demonstrate that woman shares his nature.

The distinction between male and female exists within the unity of humanity.

Biblical anthropology therefore combines common human dignity with sexual differentiation.

---

## 50. Implications for the Doctrine of Marriage

Marriage is grounded in creation.

The man recognizes the woman as his corresponding counterpart.

Verse 24 will establish the covenantal and one-flesh implications of this recognition.

The marital relationship therefore has a divine foundation.

---

## 51. Implications for the Dignity of Women

Adam does not identify the woman as an animal or lesser creature.

He identifies her as his own flesh.

The language communicates profound dignity and intimacy.

The woman belongs to the same image-bearing humanity as the man.

---

## 52. Implications for Human Relationality

Adam's joy demonstrates that human beings are created for relationship.

The problem of solitude is answered by the creation of another human person.

Relationship is therefore part of God's good design.

---

## 53. Implications for the Theology of the Body

Bone and flesh language connects personal identity with embodiment.

The woman's bodily origin matters.

Marriage's one-flesh union will also involve embodied persons.

Genesis therefore presents a deeply embodied anthropology.

---

## 54. Implications for Complementarity

The man and woman share humanity but differ sexually.

The linguistic wordplay between *ʾîš* and *ʾiššāh* reflects this relationship.

The passage therefore provides an important foundation for understanding complementarity as part of creation.

---

## 55. Canonical Significance

Genesis 2:23 directly prepares for Genesis 2:24.

It also stands behind Jesus' teaching concerning marriage and Paul's use of Genesis in Ephesians 5.

The verse is therefore foundational to later biblical theology concerning marriage and the relationship between Christ and His church.

---

## 56. Relation to the Whole Biblical Enterprise

Genesis 2:23 contributes to the Bible's larger movement from creation, through Fall, redemption, and ultimately new creation.

The original marriage relationship is later affected by sin.

Yet the creational pattern remains.

Christ redeems His people.

The church becomes associated with Christ as His bride.

The final biblical picture of redeemed humanity is therefore illuminated by the first human relationship established in Genesis.

---

## 57. Final Exegetical Reflection

Genesis 2:23 records Adam's joyful response to the woman whom God has brought to him. It is one of the earliest and most important poetic statements in Scripture, and it provides the interpretive key to the relationship between the man and the woman.

The first words are especially significant:

**“This at last.”**

Adam has just experienced the presentation of the animals.

He named them.

He observed them.

But none was found to correspond to him.

Now God brings the woman.

Adam immediately recognizes the difference.

This is not merely another creature.

This is his counterpart.

Adam's response is therefore a declaration of recognition.

He says that she is “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.”

The language expresses profound unity.

The woman is derived from Adam.

She shares his humanity.

She belongs to his kind.

She is not an animal.

She is not an unrelated being.

She is the human counterpart God promised in verse 18.

The phrase “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh” also communicates intimacy and kinship. The woman is not simply compatible with Adam in an abstract sense. She is intimately connected with him.

The statement therefore provides the foundation for the one-flesh principle of verse 24.

There is a remarkable progression in the passage.

God creates the man.

God identifies that the man should not be alone.

God brings the animals before him.

None is suitable.

God causes the man to sleep.

God takes from him.

God builds the woman.

God brings her to the man.

The man recognizes her.

The two will become one flesh.

This progression demonstrates that marriage is rooted in God's creative initiative.

Adam's response also reveals that God's provision produces joy.

The woman is not presented as a burden.

She is welcomed.

The words are celebratory.

The man recognizes the goodness of what God has done.

This is important for understanding marriage before the Fall.

Marriage belongs to the original good creation.

The distortions of marriage described elsewhere in Scripture are consequences of sin, not the original design.

The verse also establishes equality of human dignity.

The woman is bone of Adam's bones and flesh of Adam's flesh.

She is his human counterpart.

Genesis 1 has already established that male and female bear God's image.

Genesis 2 now shows how that common humanity is expressed relationally.

The man and woman are distinct.

Adam remains man.

She is woman.

Their distinction is not erased.

Yet their unity is profound.

This is one of the fundamental patterns of biblical anthropology: unity without sameness.

The naming of the woman is also significant.

Adam says she shall be called *ʾiššāh* because she was taken from *ʾîš*.

The wordplay emphasizes correspondence.

The woman belongs with the man in a way that the animals do not.

The two words are distinct but related.

This linguistic relationship reinforces the theological relationship.

Adam's naming should not be interpreted simply as an assertion of ownership.

The immediate context is one of recognition.

He identifies the woman according to her relationship to himself.

She is his corresponding female counterpart.

The verse therefore presents a remarkably positive picture of male-female relationship before sin.

There is no shame.

There is no hostility.

There is no exploitation.

There is recognition and joy.

The woman is received as God's good gift.

The verse also contributes to the biblical theology of the body.

Adam does not speak of the woman merely in abstract spiritual terms.

He speaks of bone and flesh.

Her bodily origin matters.

Their future one-flesh union will likewise involve embodied persons.

The Bible therefore presents the human body as meaningful rather than incidental.

The body belongs to God's creational design.

The Christological implications become visible when Genesis is read within the whole biblical canon.

Adam and the woman provide the foundational human marriage pattern.

Later Scripture develops marriage imagery in relation to covenant.

The prophets sometimes portray God's relationship with Israel through husband-and-wife imagery.

The New Testament identifies Christ as bridegroom and the church as His bride.

Paul's teaching in Ephesians 5 explicitly draws upon Genesis 2:24.

Thus the first marriage is not an isolated event.

It becomes part of the theological vocabulary through which Scripture describes God's covenant love.

Adam's declaration of shared flesh contributes to this pattern of profound belonging.

The comparison between Adam and Christ should be handled carefully.

Adam is the first man.

Christ is the last Adam.

Adam's story belongs to the beginning of creation.

Christ's work belongs to redemption and new creation.

The two should not be collapsed into one another, but the canonical relationship is meaningful.

Genesis establishes the first man and woman.

The New Testament develops the picture of Christ and His bride.

The Bible ultimately moves toward the marriage supper of the Lamb.

Genesis 2:23 therefore participates in the opening movement of one of Scripture's great themes.

Theologically, the verse teaches that God creates human beings for relationship, that male and female share one human nature, that sexual distinction is meaningful, that marriage belongs to the good creation, and that the woman is God's gracious provision for Adam.

It also teaches that human beings recognize and respond to God's gifts.

God creates.

Adam recognizes.

God provides.

Adam rejoices.

The passage therefore combines divine sovereignty with genuine human response.

God's work comes first.

Human recognition follows.

This pattern is significant throughout Scripture.

Genesis 2:23 is consequently much more than an ancient statement about the first woman.

It is a declaration concerning humanity itself.

The woman is bone of Adam's bones.

She is flesh of his flesh.

She is woman corresponding to man.

She is God's provision.

She is fully human.

She is fully dignified.

And she is the one with whom the man will enter the one-flesh relationship established by God.

The verse stands at the threshold between creation and covenant.

It moves from God's act of creation toward the human institution of marriage.

It establishes the language of unity that will later be used to explain one-flesh union.

And, within the whole biblical enterprise, it contributes to the imagery that ultimately points toward Christ and His bride.

**Genesis 2:23 therefore presents Adam's joyful recognition of the woman as his corresponding human counterpart, declaring through the language of “bone” and “flesh” their profound unity of human nature while preserving their distinction as man and woman, and establishing the creational foundation for marriage, human dignity, relationality, embodied identity, and the later biblical theology of Christ and His bride.**
