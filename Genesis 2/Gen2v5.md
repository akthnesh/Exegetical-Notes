# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:5

## Genesis 2:5

> **“When no bush of the field was yet in the land and no small plant of the field had yet sprung up—for the LORD God had not caused it to rain on the land and there was no man to work the ground,”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וְכֹל שִׂיחַ הַשָּׂדֶה טֶרֶם יִהְיֶה בָאָרֶץ וְכָל־עֵשֶׂב הַשָּׂדֶה טֶרֶם יִצְמָח כִּי לֹא הִמְטִיר יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים עַל־הָאָרֶץ וְאָדָם אַיִן לַעֲבֹד אֶת־הָאֲדָמָה**

## Transliteration

*Wəḵol śîaḥ haśśādeh ṭerem yihyeh ḇāʾāreṣ wəḵol-ʿēśeḇ haśśādeh ṭerem yiṣmāḥ kî lōʾ himṭîr YHWH ʾĕlōhîm ʿal-hāʾāreṣ wəʾādām ʾayin laʿăḇōḏ ʾeṯ-hāʾăḏāmāh*

## Literal Rendering

**“And every shrub of the field was not yet in the land, and every plant of the field had not yet sprouted, because the LORD God had not caused rain upon the land, and there was no man to work the ground.”**

---

## 1. “When no bush of the field was yet in the land”

Genesis 2:5 begins the focused description of the earthly setting in which humanity will appear.

The verse refers to:

**“no bush of the field”**

The Hebrew expression is:

**שִׂיחַ הַשָּׂדֶה (*śîaḥ haśśādeh*)**

The word **שִׂיחַ (*śîaḥ*)** refers to a shrub, bush, or plant growth.

The phrase **“of the field”** narrows the reference to vegetation associated with the cultivated or open land.

The statement is therefore describing a particular stage in the development of the land that will become the setting for human vocation.

The verse should be read in the context of Genesis 2 rather than as a denial of Genesis 1.

Genesis 1 has already described God creating vegetation.

Genesis 2:5 is now focusing on the particular condition of the land before the human being is placed there to cultivate and keep it.

The narrative has moved from the broad creation account to the specific environment of humanity.

---

## 2. “No small plant of the field had yet sprung up”

The verse continues:

**“and no small plant of the field had yet sprung up.”**

The Hebrew is:

**עֵשֶׂב הַשָּׂדֶה (*ʿēśeḇ haśśādeh*)**

The word **עֵשֶׂב (*ʿēśeḇ*)** commonly refers to grass, herbaceous vegetation, or plants.

The verb **יִצְמָח (*yiṣmāḥ*)** comes from **צָמַח (*ṣāmaḥ*)**, meaning **“to sprout,” “to grow,” or “to spring up.”**

The point is that this particular vegetation has not yet appeared in its described condition.

Again, the context is important.

Genesis 2:5 is preparing the reader for the appearance and vocation of Adam.

The issue is not whether God had created vegetation at all.

The issue is the condition of the field and the relationship between vegetation, rain, and human cultivation.

The narrative is establishing the setting in which Adam's work will take place.

---

## 3. “Yet” — טֶרֶם (*ṭerem*)

Twice in Genesis 2:5 the text uses:

**טֶרֶם (*ṭerem*)**

meaning:

**“not yet,” “before,” or “prior to.”**

The repetition is important.

**“No bush … was yet…”**

**“No plant … had yet…”**

The text is describing a situation that precedes something that is about to happen.

Genesis 2:5 therefore functions as a narrative preparation.

The reader is being told what has not yet happened before the introduction of the man.

This makes Adam's arrival significant.

The verse is essentially preparing the question:

**Why has this vegetation not yet appeared in this setting?**

The answer comes in the following clauses.

---

## 4. “For the LORD God had not caused it to rain”

The first explanation is:

**“for the LORD God had not caused it to rain on the land.”**

The Hebrew verb is derived from **מָטַר (*māṭar*)**, meaning **“to rain.”**

The causative form used here communicates that the LORD God had not caused or sent rain upon the land.

The statement emphasizes God's sovereignty over the natural processes that sustain the earth.

Rain is not portrayed as an autonomous force outside God's control.

The LORD God governs the conditions under which vegetation grows.

This is consistent with the broader biblical worldview.

The natural world is dependent upon God's providential rule.

---

## 5. Divine providence and the created environment

Genesis 2:5 provides an early picture of providence.

God created the world, but the created world also depends upon His continued ordering and provision.

The land requires water.

Vegetation requires suitable conditions.

Human labor will soon become part of the cultivation of the ground.

The verse therefore begins to reveal a network of relationships:

**God → rain → land → vegetation → humanity's labor.**

The created order is interconnected.

Yet all of these relationships remain under God's sovereign authority.

The LORD God is not merely the Creator of the initial world.

He is also the One who governs its conditions.

---

## 6. “And there was no man”

The second explanation is:

**“and there was no man to work the ground.”**

The Hebrew expression is:

**וְאָדָם אַיִן (*wəʾādām ʾayin*)**

Literally:

**“and man was not”**

or:

**“and there was no man.”**

This is a significant statement because the narrative is about to introduce Adam.

The absence of the man is directly connected with the condition of the ground.

Humanity has a God-given role within creation.

The land is not simply a passive background.

It is the sphere in which Adam will exercise his vocation.

Genesis 2:5 therefore prepares for the formation of the man in verses 7 and following.

---

## 7. “To work the ground” — לַעֲבֹד אֶת־הָאֲדָמָה (*laʿăḇōḏ ʾeṯ-hāʾăḏāmāh*)

The final phrase is:

**“to work the ground.”**

The Hebrew uses the verb:

**עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*)**

which can mean:

**“to work,” “to serve,” or “to labor.”**

The noun:

**אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*)**

means:

**“ground,” “soil,” or “land.”**

The relationship between **אָדָם (*ʾādām*)** and **אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*)** is significant.

The man is closely associated linguistically with the ground from which he will be formed.

The narrative emphasizes humanity's earthly creatureliness.

Adam comes from the ground and is appointed to work the ground.

Yet the ground itself ultimately belongs to God because God created it.

---

## 8. Adam and the ground

The wordplay between:

**אָדָם (*ʾādām*) — man**

and

**אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmāh*) — ground**

becomes increasingly important in Genesis.

Genesis 2:7 will state that the LORD God formed the man from the dust of the ground.

The relationship is therefore:

**ground → man → work of the ground.**

Adam is both dependent upon the ground and responsible for cultivating it.

This provides a biblical foundation for understanding humanity as an earthly creature entrusted with a vocation.

Human beings are not divine beings temporarily inhabiting bodies.

They are creatures formed by God from the earth.

---

## 9. Work existed before the Fall

Genesis 2:5 is especially important for understanding the biblical doctrine of work.

The man is created to **work the ground**.

This occurs before Genesis 3.

Therefore, work itself is not a result of sin.

The Fall will introduce painful toil, frustration, and cursed ground.

But meaningful labor belongs to God's original design.

Adam's work is part of his vocation as God's image-bearer.

This corrects the idea that all human labor is inherently a consequence of the Fall.

Work is good.

What the Fall corrupts is the experience and conditions of work.

---

## 10. The relationship between Genesis 1 and Genesis 2

Genesis 2:5 should be read carefully alongside Genesis 1.

Genesis 1:11–12 describes God commanding the earth to produce vegetation.

Genesis 2:5 then says that particular “bush of the field” and “plant of the field” had not yet sprung up in the described setting.

These statements need not be contradictory.

Genesis 1 presents the broad sequence of God's creation.

Genesis 2 narrows the focus to the human environment.

The vocabulary also differs.

Genesis 1 speaks broadly about vegetation produced by the earth.

Genesis 2:5 speaks specifically about vegetation of **“the field”** and connects its condition with rain and human cultivation.

The second account therefore focuses on the agricultural environment associated with humanity's vocation.

---

## 11. Rain and irrigation

Genesis 2:5 introduces rain as a factor in the condition of the land.

The verse does not provide a complete meteorological description of the antediluvian world.

It simply states that the LORD God had not caused rain upon the land at this stage of the narrative.

The text should therefore not be made to answer questions that it does not explicitly address.

Its immediate purpose is theological and narrative:

**God controls the provision necessary for the land, and humanity has not yet been placed there to work it.**

Genesis 2:6 will introduce another means by which the ground is watered, continuing the description of the environment.

---

## 12. God's sovereignty over rain

Rain is repeatedly presented elsewhere in Scripture as belonging to God's providential government.

The LORD gives rain.

The LORD withholds rain.

The LORD sends rain upon the earth.

Genesis 2:5 establishes this principle at the beginning of biblical history.

The fertility of the land is not ultimately autonomous.

The earth depends upon God's provision.

This becomes especially significant in the Old Testament, where rain and agricultural fertility are closely connected with God's covenant blessings and judgments.

---

## 13. Humanity as God's appointed cultivator

The final phrase of Genesis 2:5 anticipates the command and vocation given to Adam.

The man is not merely placed in Eden to consume its resources.

He is placed there to work and keep it.

Human dominion therefore involves responsible cultivation.

Genesis 1:28 describes humanity's dominion over creation.

Genesis 2:5 begins showing what that dominion looks like in practice.

It involves labor.

It involves care.

It involves cultivation.

It involves stewardship.

Human authority over creation is therefore not permission for exploitation.

It is a responsibility delegated by the Creator.

---

## 14. The theological significance of the ground

The ground has an important role throughout Genesis.

It provides food.

It receives human labor.

It becomes associated with blessing and curse.

After the Fall, the ground becomes a central part of God's judgment upon Adam.

Later, the ground receives the blood of Abel.

The land becomes central to God's promises to Abraham.

Genesis 2:5 therefore introduces a theme that will become increasingly important:

**humanity's relationship to the ground is inseparable from humanity's relationship to God.**

The ground is God's creation.

Human beings work it under God's authority.

---

## 15. The LORD God and human vocation

The repeated use of **YHWH Elohim** in Genesis 2 emphasizes that the God who creates also personally assigns vocation.

God does not merely create Adam and leave him without purpose.

The human vocation comes from God.

The man will receive a place.

He will receive work.

He will receive boundaries.

He will receive a command.

He will receive a companion.

Genesis 2 therefore presents human life as purposeful because it is established by the LORD God.

---

## 16. Genesis 2:5 and human dependence

Genesis 2:5 shows that both land and humanity are dependent.

The land depends upon water.

Humanity depends upon God for life.

Humanity depends upon the ground for food.

The ground depends upon God's providential ordering.

This creates a theology of dependence rather than autonomy.

Human beings may cultivate the earth, but they do not create the earth.

They may plant, but God ultimately governs growth.

They may work, but they remain dependent creatures.

This theme appears again in the New Testament when Paul describes planting and watering while affirming that God gives the growth.

---

## 17. Genesis 2:5 and stewardship

The verse contributes to a biblical theology of stewardship.

Human beings are given responsibility over creation, but they do not possess absolute ownership.

The ground belongs to God.

The man works the ground under God's authority.

This establishes a fundamental principle:

**human dominion is delegated dominion.**

The proper response to creation is therefore neither worship of nature nor careless exploitation.

It is faithful stewardship before the Creator.

---

## 18. Genesis 2:5 and the coming Fall

Genesis 2:5 is still located in the pre-Fall world.

The ground has not yet been cursed because of Adam's sin.

Work has not yet become painful toil.

Human vocation has not yet been frustrated by thorns and thistles.

This distinction is essential.

Genesis 2 presents work as part of the good creation.

Genesis 3 presents the corruption of that work.

The Fall therefore does not create human vocation.

It distorts it.

The gospel's redemptive work ultimately addresses not only individual guilt but the wider consequences of sin upon creation and human labor.

---

## 19. Genesis 2:5 and Christ

Genesis 2:5 does not explicitly mention Christ.

Nevertheless, its theology of creation, human vocation, and the ground contributes to themes that the New Testament develops in relation to Christ.

The New Testament identifies Christ as the agent through whom all things were created.

Christ is also presented as the one through whom creation is being reconciled and ultimately renewed.

Romans 8 describes creation as subjected to frustration because of the Fall and awaiting liberation.

Thus the problem introduced after Genesis 2 will eventually become part of the redemptive mission accomplished by Christ.

Genesis 2:5 belongs to the creation foundation of that larger story.

---

## 20. Literary function within Genesis 2

Genesis 2:5 is a **preparatory verse**.

It establishes three conditions:

**The field vegetation described has not yet appeared.**

**The LORD God has not caused rain upon the land.**

**There is no man to work the ground.**

These conditions prepare for what follows.

Genesis 2:7 will introduce the formation of man.

Genesis 2:8 will introduce the garden.

Genesis 2:15 will explicitly describe Adam's work.

The narrative therefore moves logically:

**Need → man created → man placed → man commissioned.**

Genesis 2:5 is the first stage of that movement.

---

## 21. The central theological proposition

Genesis 2:5 can be summarized in this central proposition:

**Before the man was formed and commissioned to cultivate the ground, God had not yet provided the conditions described for the field vegetation; the verse therefore prepares for humanity's creation and God-given vocation within the created order.**

Or more simply:

**God prepares the world for the man whom He will create and commission to work the ground.**

The verse therefore connects creation with vocation.

---

## Exegetical Outline

**Genesis 2:5**

**“No bush of the field was yet in the land”**

→ The narrative describes a particular stage of the human environment.

→ The focus has narrowed from cosmic creation to the field.

**“No small plant of the field had yet sprung up”**

→ The field vegetation had not yet appeared in the described condition.

→ The statement prepares for the events that follow.

**“The LORD God had not caused it to rain”**

→ God governs the conditions necessary for the land.

→ Rain is subject to divine providence.

**“There was no man”**

→ Humanity has not yet appeared in the narrative.

→ The absence of man is directly connected with the next stage.

**“To work the ground”**

→ Humanity is created for meaningful vocation.

→ Work belongs to God's good design before the Fall.

→ Adam's relationship with the ground anticipates his formation from it.

**Central idea:**

**Genesis 2:5 prepares for the creation of Adam by describing the undeveloped condition of the field and emphasizing that the LORD God governs the land while preparing humanity for its God-given vocation to cultivate the ground.**

---

## Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:5 establishes or reinforces these major doctrines:

1. **Divine providence** — God governs the natural conditions of the earth.
2. **Creation** — the land and its vegetation belong to God's created order.
3. **Human vocation** — work is part of God's original design.
4. **Stewardship** — humanity is appointed to cultivate and care for creation.
5. **Human dependence** — humanity depends upon God and the created order He sustains.
6. **Creator–creature distinction** — humans work the ground but do not own creation absolutely.
7. **Creation's goodness** — work and cultivation precede the Fall.
8. **Human creatureliness** — Adam's identity is closely connected with the ground from which he will be formed.
9. **Divine sovereignty** — rain and fertility are ultimately under God's authority.
10. **Biblical anthropology** — humanity is both earthly and vocational.
11. **The theology of work** — labor is good in itself, while the Fall introduces frustration and toil.
12. **Redemptive history** — the condition of creation prepares the setting for the Fall and the later need for redemption.
13. **Christological fulfillment** — the New Testament places creation and its eventual renewal within the work and lordship of Christ.

---

## A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

Genesis 2:5 can be structured around three propositions:

### I. THE CREATION IS DEPENDENT UPON GOD

**“The LORD God had not caused it to rain…”**

The land depends upon God's providential provision.

### II. HUMANITY IS DEPENDENT UPON GOD

**“…and there was no man…”**

The man has not yet been formed; his existence will come from God's creative action.

### III. HUMANITY IS CREATED FOR RESPONSIBLE WORK

**“…to work the ground.”**

Human labor belongs to God's original good design.

**Main proposition:**

**Genesis 2:5 teaches that the land and its conditions remain under God's providential control while the absence of humanity anticipates the creation of Adam and his God-given vocation to cultivate the ground.**

The canonical movement can be summarized:

**Genesis 1:11–12**  
God causes the earth to produce vegetation.

**Genesis 2:5**  
The narrative focuses upon the field and the conditions preceding Adam's vocation.

**Genesis 2:7**  
God forms Adam from the dust of the ground.

**Genesis 2:15**  
God places Adam in Eden to work and keep it.

**Genesis 3:17–19**  
Sin brings curse and painful toil upon the ground.

**Romans 8:19–23**  
Creation awaits liberation from the effects of the Fall.

**Colossians 1:16–20**  
Christ is the Creator and the reconciler of all things.

**Revelation 21:1–5**  
God brings His redemptive purposes to consummation in the renewed creation.

Genesis 2:5 therefore stands at the threshold of humanity's vocation.

The land awaits its cultivator.

The man has not yet appeared.

The rain remains under God's sovereign control.

And the narrative is about to reveal the Creator forming the man from the very ground he will be called to work.

**The God who created the ground creates the man, gives him his vocation, and remains sovereign over the world in which that vocation is carried out.**
