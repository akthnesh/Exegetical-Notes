# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 2:15

## Genesis 2:15

> **“The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it.”**

— ESV

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּקַּח יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאָדָם וַיַּנִּחֵהוּ בְגַן־עֵדֶן לְעָבְדָהּ וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ**

## Transliteration

*Wayyiqqaḥ YHWH ʾĕlōhîm ʾet-hāʾādām wayyanniḥēhû bəgan-ʿēden ləʿovdāh ûləšomrāh.*

## Literal Rendering

**“And the LORD God took the man and caused him to rest in the garden of Eden, to work it and to keep it.”**

---

## 1. The Verse in Its Immediate Context

Genesis 2:15 describes God's placement of the man within the garden of Eden and introduces the man's assigned vocation.

The preceding verses describe the garden's environment. God planted the garden, caused trees to grow that were pleasant to the sight and good for food, and provided the river that watered the garden. Genesis 2:14 identifies two of the rivers as the Tigris and the Euphrates.

Now the narrative turns directly to the man.

The LORD God takes the man and places him in Eden.

The purpose is expressed through two infinitives:

**to work it**

and

**to keep it.**

This is the first explicit description of humanity's vocational responsibility within the garden.

---

## 2. “The LORD God Took the Man”

The verse begins:

**וַיִּקַּח יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאָדָם**

The subject is **the LORD God**.

The verb is **לָקַח (*lāqaḥ*)**, meaning **“to take.”**

The man does not independently establish himself in Eden.

God takes him.

This emphasizes divine initiative.

The man's position within the garden is not the result of human achievement.

It is an appointment established by God.

---

## 3. Divine Initiative

The repeated actions of God throughout Genesis 2 are significant.

God forms the man.

God plants the garden.

God causes the trees to grow.

God provides the river.

God takes the man.

God places the man in the garden.

The narrative consistently presents God as the active giver and organizer of humanity's environment.

Human vocation follows divine provision.

---

## 4. “The Man”

The Hebrew expression is:

**הָאָדָם (*hāʾādām*)**

meaning:

**“the man.”**

The word is closely related to **אֲדָמָה (*ʾădāmâ*)**, “ground” or “soil.”

The relationship between Adam and the ground is significant within Genesis 2.

The man is formed from the dust of the ground.

He is therefore both dependent upon God and intimately connected with the created earth.

Genesis 2:15 continues this relationship by assigning him responsibility for cultivating the garden.

---

## 5. Adam and the Ground

The man comes from the ground.

He lives from the ground.

He is assigned to work the ground.

Later, because of sin, the ground becomes subject to God's curse.

Finally, the man returns to the ground in death.

The biblical narrative therefore connects humanity with the earth in a profound way.

Genesis 2:15 stands within that theology by presenting agricultural labor as part of humanity's original calling.

---

## 6. “And Put Him”

The second major action is:

**וַיַּנִּחֵהוּ**

The verb is related to **נוּחַ (*nûaḥ*)**, carrying the idea of rest, settling down, or giving a place of rest.

The LORD God causes the man to be settled in the garden.

The idea is more than simply physical relocation.

God establishes the man within a prepared environment.

He gives him a place.

He gives him a task.

He gives him a home in which to exercise his vocation.

---

## 7. “In the Garden of Eden”

The man is placed:

**בְגַן־עֵדֶן**

**“in the garden of Eden.”**

Eden is the environment prepared by God.

The garden has already been described as a place of beauty, nourishment, water, and abundance.

The man's work therefore occurs within an environment that God has already prepared.

This is important for understanding the biblical doctrine of work.

Work does not begin as a punishment.

Work exists before the Fall.

---

## 8. Work Before the Fall

One of the most important observations from Genesis 2:15 is that labor is part of God's original good creation.

Adam is given work before Genesis 3.

Therefore, work itself is not a consequence of sin.

The curse affects the character and difficulty of labor, but it does not create the basic human vocation of productive work.

Human beings were designed to exercise responsible activity within God's creation.

---

## 9. “To Work It”

The first purpose phrase is:

**לְעָבְדָהּ**

from the Hebrew verb:

**עָבַד (*ʿābad*)**

meaning **“to work,” “to serve,” or “to labor.”**

The object is feminine because it refers to the garden.

Adam is commanded to work the garden.

The language establishes productive activity as part of human life in Eden.

---

## 10. The Meaning of ʿAbad

The verb **ʿābad** has a broad semantic range.

It can refer to ordinary labor or agricultural work.

It can also be used elsewhere in Scripture for serving or worshiping God.

The immediate context of Genesis 2:15 concerns cultivating and working the garden.

Therefore, the primary meaning here should be understood in relation to agricultural stewardship.

Nevertheless, the broader range of the verb allows an important theological observation: human labor can be understood as service performed within God's creation.

---

## 11. Work as Service

Biblical work is not inherently opposed to worship.

Human beings serve God partly through faithful activity in the world He has made.

Adam's work in Eden is performed under God's command.

The garden belongs to God.

The task comes from God.

The ability to perform the task comes from God.

Thus work becomes an expression of creaturely service.

---

## 12. Work Is a Gift

Genesis 2:15 presents work before sin.

This means that productive labor belongs to the goodness of creation.

Human beings are not created merely to consume.

They are created to cultivate.

They are not merely receivers.

They are also workers.

God provides the garden, and humanity is given responsibility within it.

This pattern establishes a foundation for a biblical theology of vocation.

---

## 13. Work and Human Dignity

Because work is part of humanity's original calling, meaningful work possesses dignity.

The gardener is not inferior to the scholar simply because gardening involves physical labor.

Adam's first recorded vocation involves tending the environment.

Physical work is therefore not beneath humanity.

It belongs to humanity's God-given purpose.

This has important implications for understanding agriculture, craftsmanship, stewardship, and other forms of productive labor.

---

## 14. The Garden Must Be Worked

Although Eden is abundant, Adam is still commanded to work it.

This demonstrates that abundance does not eliminate vocation.

God's provision does not mean human beings have nothing to do.

Instead, God's provision creates the environment in which meaningful work can occur.

The garden is productive.

Adam participates in maintaining and cultivating that productivity.

---

## 15. Work and Stewardship

The second purpose phrase is:

**וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ**

from:

**שָׁמַר (*šāmar*)**

meaning:

**“to keep,” “guard,” “watch,” or “preserve.”**

The word introduces the complementary responsibility of guarding the garden.

Adam is not merely a producer.

He is also a keeper.

He must cultivate and protect.

---

## 16. “To Keep It”

The ESV translates *šāmar* as:

**“to keep it.”**

The term can involve watching over something carefully.

This means Adam's responsibility includes preservation.

The garden is God's provision, but Adam is entrusted with its care.

This establishes an early biblical foundation for stewardship.

---

## 17. Work and Keep

The two verbs belong together:

**ʿābad — work**

**šāmar — keep**

The first emphasizes productive activity.

The second emphasizes protective responsibility.

Together they describe a comprehensive stewardship.

Adam is called to develop and preserve.

He must use the garden and care for it.

He must cultivate its productivity without destroying its integrity.

---

## 18. Development and Preservation

The combination of the two verbs offers a useful framework for responsible human activity.

Human beings should cultivate creation.

But cultivation must not become destruction.

Human beings should use resources.

But use must remain responsible.

Human beings should develop.

But development should include preservation.

Genesis 2:15 therefore provides a foundational principle for ecological and agricultural stewardship.

---

## 19. Humanity as Steward

Adam is not the ultimate owner of Eden.

God establishes the garden.

God places Adam within it.

God gives Adam the task.

The garden remains God's creation.

Adam therefore exercises delegated responsibility.

This distinction between ownership and stewardship is fundamental to biblical anthropology.

---

## 20. Stewardship Rather Than Absolute Ownership

The biblical concept of dominion should not be interpreted as unlimited exploitation.

Genesis 2:15 places responsibility alongside authority.

The man works the garden.

The man keeps the garden.

He is accountable to the God who entrusted it to him.

Human authority is therefore subordinate to divine authority.

---

## 21. The Garden as God's Provision

The garden was already prepared before Adam was placed within it.

This reveals a significant order:

**God provides.**

**Humanity receives.**

**Humanity works.**

**Humanity keeps.**

The man's labor is therefore responsive rather than autonomous.

He works with what God has already given.

---

## 22. Grace and Vocation

Although Genesis 2 does not use the later theological terminology of grace, the structure illustrates a principle developed throughout Scripture.

God's initiative comes first.

Human responsibility follows.

The man does not earn Eden through his work.

He receives Eden and then works within it.

The order matters.

Vocation is a response to divine generosity.

---

## 23. Work Is Not a Means of Earning God's Favor

Adam does not work in order to become worthy of Eden.

He works because God has already placed him there.

This distinction is important.

Biblical work is not presented here as a mechanism for earning God's acceptance.

It is an expression of responsibility within an already-given environment.

---

## 24. The Relationship Between Worship and Work

Because *ʿābad* can elsewhere refer to service rendered to God, Genesis 2:15 provides an important conceptual connection between labor and service.

Adam's agricultural labor takes place under God's command.

The garden is God's.

The task is God's.

The goal is God's glory.

Although the verse does not explicitly call Adam's gardening “worship,” his obedient work belongs within his service to God.

---

## 25. Vocation Before Institution

Adam's first vocation is given before the development of human institutions.

There is no city.

There is no marketplace.

There is no government.

There is no formal economy.

There is simply God, humanity, creation, and assigned responsibility.

This demonstrates that vocation is more fundamental than later economic structures.

Work belongs to human nature.

---

## 26. Agriculture and the Human Calling

Genesis 2:15 is particularly important for an agricultural theology.

The first human task described in Scripture is connected with cultivation.

Humanity is called to care for productive land.

Agriculture therefore has deep biblical significance.

Food production is not merely an economic necessity.

It participates in the original human vocation to cultivate God's creation.

---

## 27. The Dignity of Farming

The garden's first worker is Adam.

His work is commanded by God.

This provides a profound theological affirmation of agricultural labor.

Farmers, gardeners, cultivators, and agricultural workers participate in an activity that has deep roots in the creation mandate.

The work of producing food is therefore honorable.

---

## 28. Creation as a Trust

The garden is entrusted to Adam.

This anticipates a broader biblical principle.

God entrusts resources to human beings.

Human beings are accountable for how they use them.

This applies to land, animals, skills, wealth, institutions, and opportunities.

The biblical concept of stewardship therefore begins very early in Scripture.

---

## 29. Responsibility Before the Fall

Adam receives responsibility while still in an unfallen condition.

This demonstrates that responsibility itself is good.

The Fall does not create responsibility.

Instead, sin corrupts the exercise of responsibility.

The human problem is not that humanity has authority.

The problem is that humanity later seeks to exercise authority independently of God.

---

## 30. The Coming Contrast in Genesis 3

Genesis 2:15 presents harmonious work.

Genesis 3 introduces the curse.

The ground becomes difficult.

Thorns and thistles appear.

Human labor becomes painful.

Sweat becomes associated with agricultural production.

The difference is crucial.

Work existed before the Fall.

Frustration and toil in the cursed sense enter because of sin.

---

## 31. The Curse Does Not Abolish Work

After the Fall, humanity continues to work.

The curse makes labor difficult, but work remains necessary.

This confirms that the original vocation was not itself sinful.

The problem is the corruption of creation.

Redemption therefore does not require humanity to abandon meaningful work.

Instead, Christian hope ultimately includes the restoration of creation and the proper ordering of human vocation.

---

## 32. Work and the Doctrine of Creation

Genesis 2:15 contributes directly to the doctrine of creation because it shows that God created humanity with purpose.

Human beings are not accidents.

They have a task.

They have responsibility.

They have a relationship to creation.

They have accountability before God.

Human vocation is therefore grounded in God's creative design.

---

## 33. Work and Human Identity

Adam's identity is not merely biological.

He is a creature made in relation to God and creation.

He is placed somewhere.

He is given something to do.

He is responsible for something.

His vocation is part of his identity as God's image-bearing creature.

---

## 34. Work and the Image of God

Genesis 1:26–28 connects humanity's creation in God's image with dominion over creation.

Genesis 2:15 gives that dominion a concrete expression.

Adam works the garden.

Adam keeps the garden.

The broad creation mandate becomes specific in the garden.

Humanity's royal stewardship is expressed through responsible cultivation.

---

## 35. Dominion Is Service

Biblical dominion should therefore resemble God's own wise care of creation.

Adam is not called to exploit the garden.

He is called to work and keep it.

Authority becomes service.

Leadership becomes stewardship.

Dominion becomes responsibility.

This provides a corrective to any understanding of human rule that ignores care.

---

## 36. The Balance of Productivity and Preservation

The two verbs are particularly valuable together.

**Work without keeping** can become exploitation.

**Keeping without working** can become neglect.

Genesis joins the two.

Human beings are called both to cultivate and to preserve.

This balanced vision remains relevant to agriculture, environmental management, economic development, and responsible use of natural resources.

---

## 37. The Garden and Human Creativity

Cultivation requires knowledge and skill.

The gardener observes.

Plants.

Maintains.

Protects.

Harvests.

Improves.

These activities require intelligence.

Human creativity therefore belongs within God's original design.

Technology and agricultural innovation can be understood as extensions of humanity's capacity to cultivate creation when used responsibly.

---

## 38. Technology and Stewardship

Genesis 2:15 does not directly discuss technology.

However, the principle of cultivating creation can provide a theological framework for considering technological development.

Technology can help human beings cultivate, protect, and improve creation.

But technology can also become destructive when separated from moral responsibility.

The biblical pattern remains:

**work it and keep it.**

Development and preservation must remain together.

---

## 39. The Garden and Sustainability

Modern discussions of sustainability often emphasize meeting human needs while preserving resources for the future.

Genesis 2:15 predates these modern categories.

It should not be turned into a modern environmental policy statement.

Nevertheless, the command to work and keep creation provides a foundational theological principle: human use of creation carries responsibility for its care.

---

## 40. Theological Ecology

A biblical theology of creation cannot stop at “God made the world.”

It must also ask how humanity is supposed to live within that world.

Genesis 2:15 provides the answer.

Humanity is placed within creation as a responsible steward.

The earth is not disposable.

It is God's creation.

Human beings may use it, but they are accountable for how they use it.

---

## 41. The Garden and Community

Genesis 2:15 concerns Adam individually.

Later, however, humanity multiplies and develops communities.

The principle of stewardship therefore expands beyond individual responsibility.

Families, communities, institutions, governments, and societies all make decisions concerning land and resources.

The original command provides a foundational paradigm for these later responsibilities.

---

## 42. Work and Human Flourishing

The garden is a place of abundance, but Adam still has work.

This suggests that human flourishing is not merely passive enjoyment.

People are designed to contribute.

They create.

Cultivate.

Build.

Protect.

Serve.

Meaningful work is part of a flourishing human life.

---

## 43. Rest and Work

The verb used for God's placement of Adam carries a relationship to rest or settling.

This is interesting because the man is placed in the garden to work and keep it.

The biblical picture therefore does not require a conflict between rest and work.

God provides a place of rest and gives a meaningful vocation within it.

Later biblical theology will develop patterns of work and Sabbath.

---

## 44. Work Under God's Word

Adam's work is not autonomous.

God speaks.

God commands.

Adam is expected to obey.

This establishes an important principle:

**human vocation must remain under divine revelation.**

Work becomes rightly ordered when it is performed within God's moral boundaries.

---

## 45. The Coming Command

Genesis 2:15 immediately precedes God's command concerning the trees.

This literary arrangement is important.

Adam is given a vocation.

Then he receives a command.

His work therefore takes place within moral limits.

Human freedom is real but bounded.

Human responsibility is active but accountable.

---

## 46. Freedom and Boundaries

The garden provides abundance.

Yet God establishes a boundary.

Adam may enjoy God's provision, but he may not define good and evil for himself.

The proper human relationship to creation therefore includes both freedom and obedience.

Human beings may cultivate creation, but they may not become its autonomous sovereign.

---

## 47. Work and Obedience

Adam's work is an act of obedience.

God tells him what to do.

The task is therefore morally meaningful.

Human labor becomes part of faithful living when it is performed according to God's purposes.

This principle extends throughout Scripture.

Faithfulness is not restricted to explicitly religious activities.

Ordinary responsibilities matter before God.

---

## 48. The Workplace Before the Fall

Eden can be understood as humanity's first workplace.

But it is more than a workplace.

It is God's garden.

Adam's work occurs in God's presence and under God's authority.

This provides a powerful model for thinking about vocation.

The workplace is not outside God's concern.

Ordinary labor belongs within the scope of discipleship and obedience.

---

## 49. The Fall and the Distortion of Vocation

Genesis 3 changes the experience of work.

Labor becomes painful.

The ground resists.

Human productivity is frustrated.

Yet humanity remains responsible to cultivate.

The Fall therefore distorts vocation rather than eliminating it.

The biblical hope is not escape from all meaningful activity.

It is restoration.

---

## 50. Redemption and Work

The New Testament develops a theology in which believers are encouraged to work faithfully.

Paul instructs Christians to work honestly and responsibly.

The principle is consistent with Genesis.

Work is part of creaturely life.

Sin corrupts it.

Christ redeems His people.

And believers are called to live faithfully in ordinary responsibilities.

---

## 51. Christ and the Theology of Work

Jesus Himself worked within ordinary human life.

He is identified with a trade before His public ministry.

He also repeatedly uses agricultural and vocational imagery in His teaching.

His kingdom teaching includes sowers, vineyards, harvests, shepherds, builders, merchants, and servants.

The biblical story therefore continues to treat work as a meaningful sphere of human life.

---

## 52. Christ and Restoration

Christ's redemptive work addresses the effects of the Fall.

The curse introduced in Genesis is not the final word.

Christ bears the curse.

He conquers death.

He inaugurates new creation.

The ultimate Christian hope is therefore not abandonment of God's creation but its restoration.

Genesis 2:15 provides part of the foundation for understanding why creation and human vocation matter.

---

## 53. The New Creation

The biblical story ends with creation restored.

The tree of life appears again.

The river of life flows.

The curse is removed.

God's people serve Him.

The final picture is not humanity becoming useless.

It is humanity living in God's restored presence and serving Him.

The vocation of God's people reaches its proper fulfillment.

---

## 54. Interpretative Challenge: Does “Keep” Mean Guard Against Satan?

Some interpreters have connected the word *šāmar* with the later entrance of the serpent.

Because Adam is commanded to keep the garden, some have suggested that he was specifically assigned to guard it against the serpent.

This is possible as a theological implication, but the verse itself does not explicitly identify a serpent-related threat.

Therefore the primary meaning should remain the general responsibility of keeping and caring for the garden.

---

## 55. Interpretative Challenge: Priesthood in Eden

Some scholars have observed parallels between the verbs associated with Adam's work and the language later used for Levitical service in the sanctuary.

This has led to proposals that Adam's work in Eden has priestly dimensions.

Such a connection can be fruitful within biblical theology.

However, Genesis 2:15 itself does not explicitly call Adam a priest.

The interpreter should therefore distinguish textual observation from later canonical synthesis.

---

## 56. Eden as a Sanctuary Pattern

Eden contains several features later associated with sacred space.

God's presence is there.

There is ordered space.

There are trees.

There is water.

There are precious materials.

There is a human servant entrusted with responsibility.

These parallels can support a sanctuary-oriented reading of Eden.

Yet the direct meaning of Genesis 2:15 remains Adam's responsibility to work and keep the garden.

---

## 57. Adam as Representative Man

Adam's vocation is also representative.

He stands at the beginning of humanity's story.

His obedience or disobedience will have consequences beyond himself.

The New Testament later contrasts Adam with Christ.

Adam becomes associated with condemnation through disobedience.

Christ becomes associated with righteousness and life through obedience.

Genesis 2:15 therefore stands near the beginning of the Adam-Christ biblical pattern.

---

## 58. Adam and Christ

The contrast should not be forced into every detail of Genesis 2:15.

Nevertheless, the broader canonical pattern is significant.

Adam receives a task and command in God's garden.

Adam fails in obedience.

Christ comes into the world and perfectly obeys the Father's will.

Adam's disobedience contributes to the entrance of death.

Christ's obedience brings the promise of life.

The garden vocation therefore belongs to the narrative background of the gospel.

---

## 59. Obedience in the Garden and Obedience in Redemption

Adam's calling shows that humanity was created for obedient service.

Christ fulfills that human calling perfectly.

He does what Adam failed to do.

This is one reason the doctrine of Christ's active obedience is important in Christian theology.

Christ's righteousness is not merely His death.

His entire obedient life matters.

---

## 60. The Garden and Gethsemane

The canonical connection between garden settings becomes especially striking.

Adam is in a garden where he faces God's command.

He disobeys.

Jesus later prays in a garden and submits Himself to the Father's will.

The contrast is theological rather than a claim that Genesis 2:15 directly predicts Gethsemane.

Adam says, in effect, through his action, that God's command will not determine his path.

Christ submits Himself completely to the Father's will.

---

## 61. From Adam's Garden to Christ's Garden

The biblical story moves from the garden of Eden to the garden associated with Christ's burial and resurrection.

The first garden becomes the setting of humanity's fall.

The later garden becomes associated with the resurrection of Christ.

The first Adam brings death into the story.

The last Adam brings life.

Genesis 2:15 therefore stands near the beginning of a profound biblical garden motif.

---

## 62. Theological and Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 2:15 supports several major doctrines.

First, work is part of God's good creation.

Second, human vocation precedes the Fall.

Third, humanity is created to cultivate creation.

Fourth, humanity is called to preserve and protect creation.

Fifth, human authority is delegated by God.

Sixth, creation does not belong absolutely to humanity.

Seventh, productive labor possesses dignity.

Eighth, physical work is not inherently inferior to spiritual activity.

Ninth, human freedom exists within divine boundaries.

Tenth, vocation is ultimately accountable to God.

Eleventh, stewardship requires both development and preservation.

Twelfth, the effects of the Fall distort work without eliminating its goodness.

Thirteenth, redemption points toward the restoration of creation and human vocation.

Fourteenth, Adam's vocation provides part of the background for the biblical contrast between Adam and Christ.

---

## 63. Practical Implications

Genesis 2:15 provides a theological foundation for several practical principles.

### Work faithfully

Work is not merely a result of the Fall.

It is part of God's design.

### Care for creation

Human beings are responsible for the world entrusted to them.

### Reject exploitation

Dominion does not mean unrestricted destruction.

### Value ordinary labor

Agricultural, domestic, technical, and physical work can all possess genuine dignity.

### Work under God's authority

Human vocation must remain morally accountable to God.

### Recognize God's provision

Human labor begins with resources God has already provided.

### Preserve what you cultivate

Productivity and conservation belong together.

---

## 64. Application to Agriculture

Genesis 2:15 has special significance for agricultural workers.

Agriculture is not merely a modern economic sector.

Cultivating land belongs to humanity's original vocation.

Farmers participate in the production of food.

They cultivate soil.

They manage water.

They protect crops.

They steward land.

These activities correspond remarkably well with the biblical pattern of **working and keeping**.

---

## 65. Application to Agro-Entrepreneurship

The principles of Genesis 2:15 can also inform agricultural entrepreneurship.

An agro-entrepreneur should not think merely in terms of extracting profit from resources.

The biblical model emphasizes:

**cultivation,**

**productivity,**

**responsibility,**

**preservation,**

and

**stewardship.**

Business success should therefore be pursued in ways that respect the created environment and the communities dependent upon it.

---

## 66. Work and Profit

Genesis 2:15 does not directly discuss profit.

Nevertheless, it establishes productive work as good.

Economic activity can therefore be understood positively when it serves legitimate human needs and operates within moral boundaries.

Profit may be a legitimate outcome of productive enterprise.

But profit does not become the ultimate purpose of creation.

God remains the ultimate owner.

---

## 67. Stewardship and Future Generations

Keeping something implies concern for its continued condition.

This has implications for future generations.

Land should not merely be consumed.

It should be cared for.

Agricultural systems should consider soil fertility.

Water should be protected.

Natural resources should be managed responsibly.

Genesis 2:15 supplies the foundational principle even though it does not provide a modern technical system of environmental management.

---

## 68. The Balance Between Human Needs and Creation

The biblical mandate does not teach that humanity must never use creation.

Adam is explicitly told to work the garden.

Human beings are meant to use creation productively.

The command also does not justify unlimited exploitation.

Adam must keep the garden.

The biblical balance is therefore:

**use without abuse,**

**development without destruction,**

**productivity with preservation.**

---

## 69. The Spiritual Meaning of Vocation

Christian theology can therefore view vocation broadly.

A vocation is not necessarily a church position.

It can include farming.

Teaching.

Craftsmanship.

Business.

Administration.

Medicine.

Technology.

Domestic work.

Public service.

What matters is that the activity is pursued faithfully within God's moral order and for the good of others.

Genesis 2:15 establishes the foundation for this broader concept.

---

## 70. The Presence of God in Ordinary Work

Adam works in Eden under God's authority.

There is no division between “sacred” and “ordinary” work in the sense that ordinary work is outside God's concern.

God is concerned with the garden.

He is concerned with Adam's labor.

He is concerned with stewardship.

This encourages believers to understand their ordinary responsibilities as places where faithfulness to God is expressed.

---

## 71. Theological Summary

Genesis 2:15 is one of Scripture's foundational passages concerning human vocation.

The LORD God takes the man and places him in Eden.

The man does not create the garden.

He receives it.

He does not establish his own mission.

God gives him one.

He is commanded to **work** the garden and **keep** it.

These two verbs establish a comprehensive pattern of stewardship.

Human beings are called to cultivate creation and protect it.

They are called to produce and preserve.

They are called to exercise authority while remaining accountable to God.

Most importantly, the verse demonstrates that work existed before the Fall.

Labor is therefore not inherently a curse.

The curse affects the frustration, pain, resistance, and futility associated with labor after sin.

But the original vocation remains good.

Human beings were created to work.

They were created to serve.

They were created to cultivate.

They were created to keep.

---

## 72. Final Exegetical Reflection

Genesis 2:15 gives one of the clearest early descriptions of what it means to be human.

God creates the man.

God provides the garden.

God places the man there.

God gives the man a task.

This order is fundamental.

Human beings are creatures before they are workers.

They are recipients before they are cultivators.

They depend upon God before they exercise responsibility.

Adam's work therefore does not compete with God's work.

God creates the garden.

Adam cultivates it.

God provides the resources.

Adam manages them.

God establishes the environment.

Adam serves within it.

The distinction between Creator and creature remains absolute.

This is why the verse is so important for a biblical theology of work.

Work is not a punishment.

Work is not a necessary evil.

Work is not merely a consequence of human sin.

Work belongs to God's original design.

The curse changes work, but does not create it.

Before there were thorns, there was cultivation.

Before there was sweat, there was stewardship.

Before there was frustration, there was vocation.

Before sin entered the world, Adam was already working.

This gives extraordinary dignity to productive labor.

The first worker in Scripture is not a king sitting on a throne.

He is a gardener.

His workplace is a garden.

His tools are associated with cultivation.

His task is given by God.

His work is therefore honorable.

Agriculture is not presented as an inferior occupation.

It belongs to the original human calling.

This has enduring significance.

Every farmer who cultivates land, every gardener who tends plants, every agricultural worker who produces food, and every steward who cares for natural resources participates in a sphere of responsibility that reaches back to the opening chapters of Scripture.

Genesis 2:15 also gives us a necessary correction to distorted concepts of dominion.

Human beings have authority over creation, but they do not possess absolute ownership.

Adam is placed in the garden by God.

The garden belongs to God.

The task belongs to God.

Adam is a steward.

This means that authority must be exercised with responsibility.

The two verbs are essential.

Adam is to **work** the garden.

He is also to **keep** it.

He is not merely an exploiter.

He is a cultivator.

He is not merely a consumer.

He is a caretaker.

He is not merely developing resources.

He is responsible for preserving them.

The combination gives us a remarkably balanced theology of human activity.

Creation is to be cultivated, not abandoned.

Resources may be used, but not recklessly destroyed.

Human productivity is good, but it must remain responsible.

Economic development can be legitimate, but stewardship cannot be sacrificed.

Agricultural productivity is valuable, but the soil, water, plants, animals, and broader environment must be treated as God's creation.

This principle is especially relevant in a world where agricultural productivity and environmental stewardship are often presented as competing priorities.

Genesis does not require the choice.

It commands both.

**Work it.**

**Keep it.**

Develop.

Preserve.

Produce.

Protect.

The two belong together.

The verse also reveals the proper relationship between divine provision and human responsibility.

God prepares the garden before Adam works it.

This means that human labor is fundamentally responsive.

Adam works with what God has provided.

He does not create the raw material of existence.

He receives creation and serves within it.

This principle provides an important theological foundation for humility.

Whatever human beings accomplish, they accomplish within a world they did not create.

Every field depends upon soil.

Every harvest depends upon water.

Every worker depends upon life and strength.

Every technological achievement depends upon created materials and human capacities that ultimately come from God.

The proper response is therefore not pride but stewardship.

Genesis 2:15 also prepares the reader for the command that follows.

Adam receives a vocation and then receives a boundary.

He is free to work within the garden, but his freedom is not absolute.

He must remain under God's word.

This is crucial to biblical anthropology.

Human beings are neither slaves without responsibility nor autonomous sovereigns.

They are responsible creatures.

They possess meaningful freedom.

They exercise real authority.

But they remain accountable to their Creator.

The Fall will occur precisely when humanity attempts to reject this arrangement.

Adam will not be content to serve as God's steward.

Humanity will seek autonomy.

The tragedy of Genesis 3 is therefore not simply that Adam eats forbidden fruit.

It is that humanity rejects the Creator's authority.

Genesis 2:15 establishes the proper order before Genesis 3 disrupts it.

God commands.

Humanity serves.

Creation is cultivated.

Creation is kept.

This order is good.

The gospel ultimately restores what sin corrupted.

The New Testament presents Christ as the last Adam.

Where the first Adam failed in obedience, Christ obeyed.

Where Adam's disobedience contributes to death, Christ's obedience leads to life.

Where Adam is associated with the beginning of humanity's exile from God's presence, Christ inaugurates the restoration of God's people.

The connection should not be forced into every detail of Genesis 2:15.

Yet the canonical movement is unmistakable.

Genesis begins with humanity in a garden.

Sin enters.

Humanity is expelled.

The biblical story proceeds through covenant, judgment, redemption, and restoration.

At the end of Scripture, God's people are again in God's presence.

The river of life flows.

The tree of life appears.

The curse is removed.

And God's servants serve Him.

The story has moved from Eden to new creation.

The vocation of humanity has not been erased.

It has been restored.

This is important because Christian hope is not fundamentally an escape from God's creation.

It is the renewal of creation.

God's purpose is not to abandon the material world but to redeem it.

The goodness of human vocation in Genesis 2 therefore belongs to the larger hope of restoration.

The work of Adam points backward to creation.

The work of Christ points forward to redemption.

And the new creation points toward the final restoration of God's purposes.

Genesis 2:15 therefore deserves to be read slowly.

It is only one verse.

Yet it answers enormous questions.

Why do human beings work?

Because God created them for meaningful responsibility.

Why should work possess dignity?

Because God Himself assigned the first human vocation.

Why should creation be cared for?

Because humanity was commanded not only to work the garden but also to keep it.

Why is exploitation wrong?

Because human authority is delegated, not absolute.

Why should agricultural labor be valued?

Because cultivation belongs to humanity's original calling.

Why does work become painful?

Because sin introduces the curse.

Why is work nevertheless still good?

Because its foundation precedes the Fall.

Why does redemption matter to vocation?

Because Christ restores what sin has corrupted.

Why does creation matter to Christian hope?

Because Scripture ends with creation renewed rather than discarded.

Genesis 2:15 therefore provides a foundational theology of **work, stewardship, vocation, responsibility, and human dignity**.

The man is placed in the garden by God.

He is given something to cultivate.

He is given something to protect.

And he is expected to do so under God's authority.

That remains the fundamental pattern.

Human beings are God's creatures.

Creation is God's gift.

Work is God's calling.

Stewardship is humanity's responsibility.

And Christ is the Redeemer through whom the purposes of God, corrupted by sin, move toward their final restoration.

**Genesis 2:15 teaches that God placed humanity within His good creation not merely to enjoy its abundance, but to cultivate and preserve it under His authority; therefore, work is a creational gift rather than a curse, stewardship is a divinely entrusted responsibility rather than absolute ownership, and humanity's original vocation finds its ultimate hope in the redemption and restoration accomplished through Christ and consummated in the new creation.**
