# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:4

**Genesis 1:4 —** “And God saw that the light was good. And God separated the light from the darkness.”

— ESV

---

## Hebrew Text

**וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאוֹר כִּי־טוֹב וַיַּבְדֵּל אֱלֹהִים בֵּין הָאוֹר וּבֵין הַחֹשֶׁךְ**

---

## Transliteration

*Wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾet-hāʾôr kî-ṭôb wayyavdēl ʾĕlōhîm bên hāʾôr ûvên haḥōšeḵ*

---

## Literal rendering

**“And God saw the light, that it was good, and God separated between the light and between the darkness.”**

---

# 1. “And God saw” — וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים (*wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm*)

Genesis 1:4 begins:

**וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God saw…”**

The verb **וַיַּרְא (*wayyarʾ*)** comes from the Hebrew root **ראה (*r-ʾ-h*)**, meaning **“to see,” “to look at,” or “to perceive.”**

The form continues the narrative sequence established in the previous verses.

Genesis 1:3 recorded:

**“And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light.”**

Genesis 1:4 now records:

**“And God saw…”**

The sequence is important:

**God speaks → light appears → God sees.**

The creation account repeatedly emphasizes God's intentional activity.

God is not an observer discovering something that He did not know would happen.

Rather, the narrative presents Him as the sovereign Creator who evaluates what He has made according to His own perfect purpose.

---

# 2. “The light” — אֶת־הָאוֹר (*ʾet-hāʾôr*)

The object of God's observation is:

**הָאוֹר (*hāʾôr*)**

**“the light.”**

This is the same noun introduced in Genesis 1:3:

**אוֹר (*ʾôr*) — “light.”**

The definite article **הַ (*ha-*)** makes it:

**“the light.”**

The light introduced by God's command is now specifically evaluated by God.

This connects verses 3 and 4:

**Verse 3 — God creates light.**

**Verse 4 — God evaluates the light.**

The creation account therefore presents creation as intentional rather than accidental.

---

# 3. The direct-object marker — אֵת (*ʾet*)

Before **הָאוֹר (*hāʾôr*)** appears the Hebrew word:

**אֶת (*ʾet*)**

This is the definite direct-object marker.

It is not normally translated into English.

The grammatical structure is therefore:

**וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאוֹר**

Literally:

**“And God saw [direct object marker] the light.”**

The structure makes the relationship clear:

**Subject:** God

**Verb:** saw

**Object:** the light

God is evaluating the light He has created.

---

# 4. “That it was good” — כִּי־טוֹב (*kî-ṭôb*)

The next phrase is:

**כִּי־טוֹב**

*kî-ṭôb*

**“that it was good.”**

The word **טוֹב (*ṭôb*)** means:

- good
- pleasant
- beneficial
- excellent
- appropriate

Its meaning is determined by context.

Here it expresses God's positive evaluation of the light.

The text does not merely say that light exists.

It says that God declares it **good**.

This introduces one of the major recurring themes of Genesis 1:

**God's evaluation of His creation.**

---

# 5. “Good” in Genesis 1

The declaration:

**“it was good”**

becomes a repeated refrain throughout the creation account.

God repeatedly evaluates His work as good.

This teaches that creation is not an accidental or meaningless product.

It has been made according to God's wise purpose.

The goodness of creation ultimately derives from the goodness of its Creator.

Genesis therefore presents a positive doctrine of the created world.

Matter is not inherently evil.

The physical universe is not a prison from which the soul must escape.

Creation belongs to God and, as His workmanship, can rightly be called good.

---

# 6. Creation is not God, but creation is good

This distinction is important.

Genesis does not teach:

**Creation = God.**

Nor does it teach:

**Creation = evil.**

Instead:

**God is the Creator.**

**Creation is His good workmanship.**

This preserves the biblical Creator–creature distinction while avoiding the idea that physical existence is inherently evil.

The light is not divine.

The light is not worthy of worship.

But the light is **good** because God made it and declared it good.

---

# 7. “And God separated” — וַיַּבְדֵּל אֱלֹהִים (*wayyavdēl ʾĕlōhîm*)

The second major action in the verse is:

**וַיַּבְדֵּל אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyavdēl ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God separated…”**

The verb **וַיַּבְדֵּל (*wayyavdēl*)** comes from the root **בדל (*b-d-l*)**, meaning **“to separate,” “to divide,” or “to distinguish.”**

This verb becomes particularly important throughout Genesis 1.

God repeatedly establishes distinctions within creation.

He separates:

**light from darkness**

**waters above from waters below**

and later establishes the distinction between:

**sea and dry land.**

Creation is therefore characterized by divine ordering and differentiation.

---

# 8. Separation as divine ordering

Genesis 1 does not present God merely as producing objects.

He also establishes **relationships, boundaries, distinctions, and functions**.

The world God creates is an ordered world.

The separation of light from darkness demonstrates that God is establishing an environment governed by distinctions.

This is part of the movement already introduced in Genesis 1:2:

**Formlessness → order**

**Emptiness → fullness**

Genesis 1:4 specifically advances:

**undifferentiated darkness and light → ordered distinction between light and darkness.**

---

# 9. “Between the light” — בֵּין הָאוֹר (*bên hāʾôr*)

The verse uses the preposition:

**בֵּין (*bên*)**

meaning:

**“between.”**

The expression is:

**בֵּין הָאוֹר**

*bên hāʾôr*

**“between the light.”**

But the phrase continues with a deliberate parallel:

**וּבֵין הַחֹשֶׁךְ**

**“and between the darkness.”**

The Hebrew therefore emphasizes the distinction:

**between the light**

and

**between the darkness.**

The repetition gives the sentence a balanced structure.

God establishes a boundary between the two.

---

# 10. “And between the darkness” — וּבֵין הַחֹשֶׁךְ (*ûvên haḥōšeḵ*)

The word:

**הַחֹשֶׁךְ (*haḥōšeḵ*)**

means:

**“the darkness.”**

This is the same darkness introduced in Genesis 1:2.

Thus Genesis 1:4 connects directly back to the earlier description:

**Genesis 1:2 — darkness was over the face of the deep.**

**Genesis 1:3 — God commanded light.**

**Genesis 1:4 — God separated light from darkness.**

The narrative is progressively transforming the initial condition of the earth into an ordered creation.

---

# 11. Light and darkness are not equal gods

The separation of light and darkness has theological significance.

The biblical text does not present light and darkness as two independent divine forces fighting for supremacy.

There is no cosmic dualism here.

God stands above both.

He creates light.

He separates light from darkness.

Both remain within His sovereign ordering of creation.

This is fundamentally different from a worldview in which good and evil are equally eternal and equally powerful.

Genesis presents one sovereign Creator.

---

# 12. Genesis 1:4 and divine sovereignty

God's sovereignty is evident in both actions:

**God saw.**

**God separated.**

He evaluates creation.

He orders creation.

He establishes boundaries within creation.

This demonstrates that the created order does not establish its own ultimate meaning.

God gives creation its order and purpose.

The universe is therefore not autonomous.

It exists under divine authority.

---

# 13. Genesis 1:4 and God's goodness

The statement:

**“the light was good”**

is also a theological affirmation about God Himself.

God does not produce something defective and then discover its goodness accidentally.

His work reflects His wise and purposeful character.

The goodness of creation is derivative.

It is good because it has been made according to the Creator's design.

This becomes particularly important when the entire creation account is considered.

Genesis culminates in the declaration that creation is **“very good.”**

Thus the goodness declared in verse 4 is part of a developing pattern that reaches its climax in Genesis 1:31.

---

# 14. Genesis 1:4 and the doctrine of order

One of the major theological themes of Genesis 1 is that God establishes order.

Genesis 1:2 describes:

**formlessness**

**emptiness**

**darkness**

**waters**

Genesis 1:3 introduces:

**light**

Genesis 1:4 introduces:

**separation.**

This reveals a pattern:

**God creates → God orders → God distinguishes → God fills.**

Creation therefore possesses structure because God gives it structure.

---

# 15. Light and darkness as a recurring biblical motif

The distinction between light and darkness becomes one of the Bible's most important recurring images.

In Genesis 1, the immediate subject is the created order.

Later Scripture uses light and darkness in moral and spiritual contexts.

Light can signify:

- truth
- holiness
- righteousness
- life
- God's presence
- salvation

Darkness can signify:

- ignorance
- evil
- judgment
- sin
- alienation

However, these later theological uses should not be imposed prematurely upon Genesis 1:4.

The immediate meaning is God's ordering of light and darkness within creation.

The broader canonical symbolism develops from this foundational creation pattern.

---

# 16. Genesis 1:4 and John 1

The light motif becomes particularly significant when Genesis is read alongside John's Gospel.

Genesis says:

**God created light.**

John introduces the Word and says:

**“In him was life, and the life was the light of men.”**

John later identifies Jesus as:

**“the true light.”**

Thus the New Testament takes the creation theme of light and develops it into a profound Christological motif.

The physical light of creation becomes part of the biblical vocabulary through which Christ's role in salvation is described.

Again, Genesis 1:4 itself is speaking primarily about created light.

The Christological significance emerges through the canonical development of the theme.

---

# 17. Genesis 1:4 and Christological fulfillment

The New Testament presents Christ as the One through whom creation came into being.

John 1:3 says that all things were made through the Word.

Colossians 1:16 declares that all things were created through Christ and for Christ.

Therefore, the Christian reader can understand Genesis 1:4 within the broader revelation of Christ's relationship to creation.

The One who is later revealed as the **Light of the world** is also identified by the New Testament as the divine Word through whom creation was made.

This produces a rich biblical-theological relationship:

**Created light → biblical light motif → Christ the Light → consummated creation.**

---

# 18. Genesis 1:4 and the rhythm of creation

Genesis 1:4 contributes to the distinctive rhythm of the creation account.

A repeated pattern emerges:

**God speaks.**

**Something happens.**

**God evaluates it.**

**God orders or separates it.**

In this verse:

**God speaks → light appears → God sees that it is good → God separates light from darkness.**

This demonstrates that the creation account is carefully structured.

It is not simply a random collection of events.

---

# 19. Literary function within Genesis

Genesis 1:4 performs two major literary functions.

First, it confirms the success of God's command in verse 3.

Second, it prepares for the establishment of day and night.

The separation introduced here becomes explicitly connected to the naming of:

**Day**

and

**Night**

in verse 5.

Thus verse 4 forms a bridge:

**Light created → light evaluated → light separated → day and night established.**

The narrative is progressively moving toward an ordered cosmos.

---

# 20. Genesis 1:4 and the goodness of God's creation

The declaration:

**“the light was good”**

provides an important foundation for a biblical doctrine of creation.

God's creation is fundamentally purposeful.

It is not meaningless.

It is not the result of divine error.

It is not inherently evil.

It is good because it comes from the good Creator.

This goodness does not mean that every later condition of creation is morally good or that the fallen world is presently in its original state.

Rather, Genesis establishes the goodness of God's original creative work.

The later biblical doctrine of the Fall must be understood against this original goodness.

---

# 21. Genesis 1:4 and the Fall

Genesis 1:4 occurs before sin enters the biblical narrative.

At this point:

**there is no human rebellion.**

**there is no death described.**

**there is no curse.**

**there is no judgment.**

God's creation is being ordered and declared good.

This becomes important for biblical theology because the Fall does not represent the discovery that God's creation was originally defective.

Rather, sin enters a creation that God had declared good.

The gospel therefore ultimately includes not merely the salvation of individuals but the restoration and renewal of creation.

---

# 22. Creation and new creation

The biblical story begins with God creating light and separating it from darkness.

The story eventually moves toward the consummation of creation.

Revelation describes the new creation in which God's glory illuminates His people.

The Bible therefore contains a broad movement:

**Genesis — creation**

**Fall — corruption**

**Redemption — restoration**

**Revelation — new creation**

The God who establishes order at creation will ultimately bring His creation to its appointed consummation.

---

# 23. The central theological proposition

If Genesis 1:4 is reduced to its fundamental theological assertion, it could be stated this way:

**God evaluates His created light as good and sovereignly establishes the distinction between light and darkness, demonstrating His authority to give creation order, boundaries, and purpose.**

Or more simply:

**God creates what is good and orders His creation according to His sovereign wisdom.**

---

# Exegetical Outline

## Genesis 1:4

### **“And God saw”**

→ God intentionally evaluates His creation.

→ Creation is under God's sovereign inspection.

### **“That the light was good”**

→ God's creation is good.

→ Physical creation is not inherently evil.

→ The goodness of creation reflects the wisdom of its Creator.

### **“And God separated”**

→ God establishes order and distinction.

→ Creation does not organize itself independently.

### **“The light”**

→ Light is God's created work.

→ Light will become a major biblical theological motif.

### **“From the darkness”**

→ Darkness is not God's equal.

→ God exercises authority over both light and darkness.

→ The created order is established through divine distinctions.

---

# Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 1:4 establishes or contributes to at least these major doctrines:

1. **Goodness of creation** — God declares His created light good.
2. **Divine sovereignty** — God evaluates and orders creation according to His will.
3. **Creator–creature distinction** — light is created, not divine.
4. **Divine order** — God establishes distinctions and boundaries within creation.
5. **Divine wisdom** — creation is ordered according to purposeful design.
6. **Anti-dualism** — light and darkness are not equal divine powers.
7. **Moral foundation of creation** — God's original creation is presented as good.
8. **Authority of God** — God determines the structure and function of creation.
9. **Biblical doctrine of light** — light becomes an important theological motif throughout Scripture.
10. **Christological fulfillment** — the New Testament identifies Christ with the biblical theme of divine light and with the work of creation.
11. **Creation and redemption** — the biblical movement from creation to new creation ultimately includes restoration and consummation.
12. **God's purposeful design** — creation is neither meaningless nor autonomous.

---

# A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

If Genesis 1:4 were developed into a Bible study or sermon, it could be structured around three propositions:

## I. GOD DECLARES HIS CREATION GOOD

**“And God saw that the light was good…”**

The Creator evaluates His work.

What God creates is good according to His perfect purpose.

---

## II. GOD ESTABLISHES ORDER

**“And God separated…”**

God does not merely create.

He orders.

He distinguishes.

He establishes boundaries.

Creation receives its structure from its Creator.

---

## III. GOD RULES OVER LIGHT AND DARKNESS

**“…the light from the darkness.”**

Light and darkness are not competing gods.

Both remain under God's sovereign authority.

God determines their distinction and relationship.

---

# Main Proposition

**Genesis 1:4 teaches that God sovereignly evaluates His creation as good and establishes the distinction between light and darkness, demonstrating that the created order receives its goodness, structure, boundaries, and purpose from its Creator.**

The movement from Genesis 1:2 through Genesis 1:4 is therefore striking:

**Genesis 1:2**

Darkness over the deep.

**Genesis 1:3**

God speaks:

**“Let there be light.”**

**Genesis 1:4**

God sees that the light is good.

**God separates the light from the darkness.**

The darkness does not overcome God.

**God gives light.**

The creation does not establish its own order.

**God establishes the order.**

And the Creator declares His work:

**“good.”**
