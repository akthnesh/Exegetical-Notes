# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:3

**Genesis 1:3 —** “And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light.”

— ESV

---

## Hebrew Text

**וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי־אוֹר**

---

## Transliteration

*Wayyōmer ʾĕlōhîm yəhî ʾôr wayəhî-ʾôr*

---

## Literal rendering

**“And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light.”**

---

# 1. “And God said” — וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים (*wayyōmer ʾĕlōhîm*)

Genesis 1:3 begins with one of the most important recurring expressions in the creation account:

**וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyōmer ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God said…”**

The verb **וַיֹּאמֶר (*wayyōmer*)** comes from the Hebrew root **אמר (*ʾ-m-r*)**, meaning **“to say”** or **“to speak.”**

The form is a wayyiqtol form, commonly used to advance the narrative.

The subject is:

**אֱלֹהִים (*ʾĕlōhîm*) — “God.”**

The same grammatical observation made in Genesis 1:1 remains important here.

Although **ʾĕlōhîm** is plural in form, when it refers to the God of Israel it takes a singular verb.

Thus:

**אֱלֹהִים יְהִי**

God speaks as one sovereign divine subject.

The narrative therefore continues to emphasize one Creator acting with absolute authority over creation.

---

# 2. God's Word and creation

Genesis 1 introduces a profound relationship between **God's speech and creation**.

God does not need to struggle physically with the darkness.

He does not need to battle another deity.

He does not need to manipulate an independent cosmic power.

He simply speaks.

**God said.**

And creation responds.

This establishes a repeated pattern throughout Genesis 1:

**God speaks → creation responds.**

The authority of God's Word is therefore central to the creation account.

His Word is not merely communication.

His Word accomplishes His sovereign purpose.

---

# 3. “Let there be” — יְהִי (*yəhî*)

The divine command is:

**יְהִי**

*yəhî*

**“Let there be.”**

This form comes from the Hebrew verb **היה (*h-y-h*)**, meaning **“to be,” “to become,” or “to come to pass.”**

The command is remarkably brief.

God does not provide a long explanation.

He does not perform a ritual.

He does not invoke another power.

He commands:

**“Let there be light.”**

The simplicity of the command emphasizes the greatness of God's authority.

The One who created the universe does not need elaborate effort.

He speaks, and creation comes into being according to His command.

---

# 4. “Light” — אוֹר (*ʾôr*)

The object of God's command is:

**אוֹר (*ʾôr*)**

**“light.”**

Light is introduced immediately after Genesis 1:2 described darkness over the face of the deep.

The contrast is therefore striking:

**Genesis 1:2 — darkness**

**Genesis 1:3 — light**

The first recorded divine command directly addresses the darkness.

God brings light into the created order.

This demonstrates that darkness does not possess ultimate authority.

God does.

---

# 5. Light before the sun

One of the most interesting features of Genesis 1 is that light appears in verse 3, while the sun, moon, and stars are described later in verses 14–19.

This means that the narrative does not initially identify the source of the light with the sun.

The text first establishes:

**God created light.**

Only later does God appoint the heavenly lights to govern the day and night and to serve as signs for seasons, days, and years.

This emphasizes God's sovereignty over the celestial realm.

The sun itself is not divine.

The source and ordering of light ultimately depend upon God.

---

# 6. Light and the biblical theology of creation

Light becomes a major biblical theme.

Here in Genesis 1, physical light is introduced as part of God's ordering of creation.

Throughout Scripture, however, light also becomes associated with:

- God's glory
- God's presence
- truth
- righteousness
- life
- salvation

Genesis 1:3 should first be understood in its immediate context as God's creation of light.

Nevertheless, the canonical development of the theme eventually gives light profound theological significance.

The Bible's opening creation narrative therefore establishes a motif that will recur throughout Scripture.

---

# 7. “And there was light” — וַיְהִי־אוֹר (*wayəhî-ʾôr*)

The response to God's command is immediate:

**וַיְהִי־אוֹר**

*Wayəhî-ʾôr*

**“And there was light.”**

The verb:

**וַיְהִי (*wayəhî*)**

comes from the same root **היה (*h-y-h*)**.

God commands:

**יְהִי אוֹר**

“Let there be light.”

The narrative responds:

**וַיְהִי־אוֹר**

“And there was light.”

The correspondence is deliberate.

God speaks.

The commanded reality appears.

The text therefore presents creation as responding to God's authoritative Word.

---

# 8. Divine sovereignty demonstrated

Genesis 1:3 provides one of the clearest demonstrations of God's sovereign power.

God does not merely wish for light.

He commands light.

And light appears.

This teaches that God's authority over creation is absolute.

Creation does not negotiate with God.

Creation does not resist God.

Creation responds to God.

The relationship is:

**Creator → command → creation.**

The Creator stands above the created order.

---

# 9. God's Word is effective

The statement:

**“And there was light”**

demonstrates the effectiveness of God's command.

Human speech can describe reality.

God's creative Word establishes reality.

This distinction is important.

When God speaks within the creation account, His Word is performative.

He commands, and the commanded result follows.

The creation account therefore presents God's Word as:

**authoritative**

**effective**

**sovereign**

**purposeful**

This theme will continue throughout Genesis 1.

---

# 10. Genesis 1:3 and the darkness of verse 2

Verse 3 should be read directly against verse 2.

Verse 2:

**“Darkness was over the face of the deep.”**

Verse 3:

**“And God said, ‘Let there be light.’”**

The literary contrast is immediate.

**Darkness → Light**

This is the first major movement of divine ordering in the chapter.

God does not leave the creation in its initial condition.

He begins bringing order and distinction.

The narrative will continue this process through the separation of:

- light and darkness
- waters above and waters below
- sea and dry land
- day and night

God creates a world characterized by ordered distinctions.

---

# 11. Light and separation

The creation of light in verse 3 prepares for the separation described in verse 4.

Genesis 1:4 says that God saw the light and separated the light from the darkness.

Thus verse 3 introduces the element that God will subsequently distinguish.

The sequence is:

**God creates light → God evaluates the light → God separates light from darkness.**

This demonstrates that creation is not random.

God creates according to an intentional order.

---

# 12. Genesis 1:3 and God's goodness

The next verse will say:

**“And God saw that the light was good.”**

This is significant for understanding verse 3.

The light God creates is part of the good creation that He is forming.

The biblical account does not portray matter as inherently evil.

God's physical creation is capable of being declared good because it comes from the good Creator.

This becomes an important foundation for biblical theology.

Creation is not divine.

But creation is also not inherently evil.

It is God's good creation.

---

# 13. Genesis 1:3 and creation ex nihilo

Genesis 1:3 contributes to the biblical doctrine of God's sovereign creation.

God commands light into existence.

The verse itself does not settle every philosophical question concerning the precise mechanics of creation from nothing.

Nevertheless, in the broader canonical context, the divine command demonstrates that God is the ultimate source and sovereign author of creation.

Hebrews 11:3 later explains that the visible world was not made from things that are visible.

John 1:3 declares:

**“All things were made through him.”**

Romans 4:17 describes God as the One who calls into existence things that do not exist.

Thus Genesis 1:3 belongs within the wider biblical testimony that creation depends upon God's sovereign action.

---

# 14. Genesis 1:3 and John 1

Genesis 1:3 also has a profound canonical relationship with John 1.

Genesis says:

**“And God said…”**

John begins:

**“In the beginning was the Word…”**

John then declares that all things were made through the Word.

The connection is significant.

Genesis presents creation occurring through God's authoritative speech.

John reveals the eternal **Word** through whom all things were made.

The New Testament therefore gives a fuller Christological understanding of God's creative activity.

The pattern can be summarized:

**Genesis 1 — God speaks and creation comes into being.**

**John 1 — the eternal Word is the agent through whom all things were made.**

---

# 15. Genesis 1:3 and Christ

The New Testament explicitly identifies Christ with God's creative work.

John 1:3 teaches that all things were made through Him.

Colossians 1:16 states that all things were created through Christ and for Christ.

Hebrews 1:2 identifies the Son as the One through whom God made the world.

Therefore, Christians can read Genesis 1:3 within the fuller revelation of Scripture and recognize that the Son participated in the divine work of creation.

This does not mean that Genesis 1:3 explicitly names Jesus Christ.

Rather, the doctrine emerges through **canonical and progressive revelation**.

Genesis establishes the creation account.

The New Testament identifies the Son's role within that creation.

---

# 16. Genesis 1:3 and the Trinity

Genesis 1:3 presents:

**God speaking.**

Genesis 1:2 presents:

**the Spirit of God hovering over the waters.**

John 1 later presents:

**the eternal Word through whom all things were made.**

The complete biblical picture therefore reveals:

**Father — Creator**

**Son — Creator**

**Spirit — active in creation**

Genesis 1:3 alone does not present the complete doctrine of the Trinity.

Nevertheless, when read canonically, the verse contributes to the biblical framework through which Christians understand the triune God as the one sovereign Creator.

---

# 17. Light as a biblical theological theme

The creation of light becomes the beginning of an extensive biblical motif.

Later Scripture uses light to communicate realities such as:

**God's glory**

**God's truth**

**God's salvation**

**righteousness**

**life**

**Christ**

**the gospel**

John's Gospel is especially significant.

John describes Christ as:

**“the light of men.”**

Jesus later declares:

**“I am the light of the world.”**

The connection should not erase the literal meaning of Genesis 1:3.

Genesis is first describing God's creation of light.

But the canonical development of the theme demonstrates how creation imagery becomes incorporated into the Bible's larger theology of redemption.

---

# 18. Creation and new creation

The theme of light also participates in the broader biblical movement from creation to new creation.

Genesis begins with:

**darkness**

and God's command:

**“Let there be light.”**

The biblical story ultimately moves toward the consummation of creation.

Revelation describes the new creation in which God's glory provides illumination.

Thus Scripture presents a broad theological trajectory:

**Creation → Light → Redemption → New Creation**

The God who commanded light into the original creation is also the God who will bring His purposes for creation to their final consummation.

---

# 19. Literary function within Genesis

Genesis 1:3 begins the sequence of divine creative commands that structures the remainder of the chapter.

The recurring formula is:

**“And God said…”**

Genesis 1:3 establishes this pattern.

God speaks.

Creation responds.

God evaluates.

God separates or orders.

The creation narrative therefore emphasizes the sovereign authority of God's speech.

The verse is not merely reporting the existence of light.

It is showing **how God's creative authority operates**.

---

# 20. The central theological proposition

If Genesis 1:3 is reduced to its fundamental theological assertion, it can be stated this way:

**The sovereign God brings light into creation by His authoritative Word, demonstrating that creation responds to His command and remains entirely subject to His will.**

Or more simply:

**God speaks, and creation obeys.**

This is one of the fundamental theological patterns of Genesis 1.

---

# Exegetical Outline

## Genesis 1:3

### **“And God said”**

→ God is the sovereign speaker.

→ Creation is subject to God's Word.

→ God's Word is authoritative and effective.

### **“Let there be”**

→ God commands reality into existence.

→ His creative authority requires no external power.

### **“Light”**

→ God introduces light into the previously dark creation.

→ Darkness does not possess ultimate authority.

→ Light becomes a major biblical theological motif.

### **“And there was light”**

→ God's command accomplishes its intended result.

→ Creation responds to the Creator.

→ God's Word is effective.

---

# Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 1:3 establishes or contributes to at least these major doctrines:

1. **Divine sovereignty** — God exercises absolute authority over creation.
2. **Divine omnipotence** — God's command accomplishes His purpose.
3. **Authority of God's Word** — God's speech is effective and sovereign.
4. **Creation** — light exists because God commands it.
5. **Creator–creature distinction** — God commands; creation responds.
6. **Order in creation** — God begins transforming darkness into an ordered creation.
7. **Goodness of creation** — the light will be declared good.
8. **Divine wisdom** — creation proceeds according to God's intentional design.
9. **Holy Spirit's activity** — verse 3 follows immediately after the Spirit's presence in verse 2.
10. **Christological fulfillment** — the New Testament identifies the Son as the eternal Word through whom all things were made.
11. **Trinitarian framework** — Father, Son, and Spirit are revealed in the complete biblical doctrine of creation.
12. **Light as theological motif** — physical light becomes foundational to later biblical imagery concerning God's glory, truth, salvation, life, and Christ.

---

# A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

If Genesis 1:3 were developed into a Bible study or sermon, it could be structured around three propositions:

## I. GOD SPEAKS

**“And God said…”**

Creation begins to respond to the authoritative Word of God.

God does not struggle with creation.

He speaks.

---

## II. GOD COMMANDS LIGHT

**“Let there be light…”**

The first recorded creative command addresses the darkness.

God brings light where darkness previously prevailed.

---

## III. CREATION OBEYS

**“…and there was light.”**

The command produces exactly what God commanded.

God speaks.

Creation responds.

The Word of the Creator is effective.

---

# Main Proposition

**Genesis 1:3 teaches that the sovereign God brings light into the darkness by His authoritative Word, demonstrating His absolute creative power and establishing the biblical pattern that creation exists and operates according to His sovereign command.**

And the movement from Genesis 1:2 to Genesis 1:3 is profound:

**Genesis 1:2**

Darkness.

**Genesis 1:3**

God speaks.

**Genesis 1:3**

Light.

The darkness does not speak.

The waters do not speak.

The deep does not speak.

**God speaks.**

And when God speaks:

**“there was light.”**
