# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:2

**Genesis 1:2 —** “The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters.”

— ESV

---

## Hebrew Text

**וְהָאָרֶץ הָיְתָה תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ  
וְחֹשֶׁךְ עַל־פְּנֵי תְהוֹם  
וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶפֶת עַל־פְּנֵי הַמָּיִם**

---

## Transliteration

*Wəhāʾāreṣ hāyətā tōhû wābōhû  
wəḥōšeḵ ʿal-pənê təhôm  
wərûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm mərāḥep̄eṯ ʿal-pənê hammāyim*

---

## Literal rendering

**“And the earth was formless and empty, and darkness was over the face of the deep, and the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters.”**

---

# 1. “And the earth” — וְהָאָרֶץ (*wəhāʾāreṣ*)

Genesis 1:2 begins with:

**וְהָאָרֶץ (*wəhāʾāreṣ*)**

Literally:

**“And the earth…”**

The conjunction **וְ (*wə-*)** connects verse 2 with the preceding declaration of Genesis 1:1.

Genesis 1:1 announced:

**“God created the heavens and the earth.”**

Genesis 1:2 now begins describing the condition of the earth within the creation account.

The noun **אֶרֶץ (*ʾereṣ*)** is the same basic word encountered in Genesis 1:1. Depending upon context, it can refer to:

- earth
- land
- ground
- territory

Here the context is cosmic and creation-oriented.

The narrative has moved from the comprehensive declaration of creation in verse 1 to the particular condition of the earth in verse 2.

This gives the opening of Genesis a deliberate movement:

**God created the heavens and the earth → the earth was…**

The text therefore begins describing the created world that God will subsequently order, fill, separate, and populate.

---

# 2. “Was” — הָיְתָה (*hāyətā*)

The verb is:

**הָיְתָה (*hāyətā*)**

from the Hebrew root **היה (*h-y-h*)**, meaning **“to be,” “to become,” or “to come to pass,”** depending upon context.

Here it is the feminine singular form, agreeing with **הָאָרֶץ (*hāʾāreṣ*)**, which is grammatically feminine.

Thus:

**הָאָרֶץ הָיְתָה**

*Hāʾāreṣ hāyətā*

**“The earth was…”**

The verb introduces the condition of the earth at this point in the narrative.

The text does not introduce another creator.

It does not describe a competing deity.

It does not describe an independent force challenging God.

Instead, the earth is simply presented as part of the created order whose condition will be transformed through God's subsequent creative commands.

---

# 3. “Without form and void” — תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ (*tōhû wābōhû*)

One of the most important expressions in Genesis 1:2 is:

**תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ**

*tōhû wābōhû*

Traditionally:

**“formless and void.”**

The two words are closely associated.

### תֹהוּ (*tōhû*)

*Tōhû* can carry ideas such as:

- formlessness
- wasteness
- emptiness
- desolation
- unproductive or uninhabited condition

### בֹהוּ (*bōhû*)

*Bōhû* occurs much less frequently in the Old Testament and is associated with emptiness or desolation.

Together:

**תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ**

describes an earth that has not yet been ordered and populated for the purposes described in the following verses.

The expression should therefore not automatically be understood as saying that God created something evil.

The text does not say:

**“The earth was sinful.”**

Nor does it say:

**“The earth was chaotic because another god opposed God.”**

Instead, the narrative presents the earth in an unformed and unfilled condition that God will subsequently address.

---

# 4. Formlessness and emptiness

The expression can be understood through two complementary ideas:

**Formlessness** — the world has not yet been ordered into its appointed distinctions.

**Emptiness** — the world has not yet been filled with the creatures that will inhabit it.

This becomes especially significant when the rest of Genesis 1 is considered.

God progressively brings:

**form → order**

and

**emptiness → fullness.**

The creation narrative therefore moves toward completion.

The earth will receive:

- light
- separated waters
- dry land
- vegetation
- heavenly lights
- sea creatures
- birds
- land animals
- humanity

Thus Genesis 1:2 describes the initial condition from which the divine ordering work proceeds.

---

# 5. “And darkness” — וְחֹשֶׁךְ (*wəḥōšeḵ*)

The next major expression is:

**וְחֹשֶׁךְ**

*wəḥōšeḵ*

**“and darkness.”**

The noun **חֹשֶׁךְ (*ḥōšeḵ*)** means **darkness**.

Darkness is introduced before the creation of light in verse 3.

This is significant because Genesis does not portray darkness as a deity or independent cosmic power.

Darkness is part of the created scene over which God exercises complete authority.

The narrative will immediately demonstrate this when God says:

**“Let there be light.”**

Darkness therefore cannot resist God's command.

God does not struggle against darkness as though the two were equal opposing forces.

Instead:

**God speaks → light appears.**

The sovereignty of God is absolute.

---

# 6. “Over the face” — עַל־פְּנֵי (*ʿal-pənê*)

The phrase:

**עַל־פְּנֵי**

*ʿal-pənê*

means literally:

**“upon/over the face of.”**

The word **פָּנִים (*pānîm*)** ordinarily means **“face”** or **“presence,”** though its plural form is commonly used.

Here it describes the surface or visible aspect of the deep.

The same expression occurs later in the verse concerning the waters:

**“over the face of the waters.”**

This creates a deliberate parallel:

**darkness over the face of the deep**

and

**the Spirit of God hovering over the face of the waters.**

The verse therefore establishes a setting dominated by darkness and waters before God begins His ordering work through His Word.

---

# 7. “The deep” — תְּהוֹם (*təhôm*)

The Hebrew word is:

**תְּהוֹם (*təhôm*)**

commonly translated:

**“the deep.”**

It refers to the vast deep waters.

This is an important feature of the creation scene.

The biblical text does not introduce **Təhôm** as a god.

The deep is not divine.

It is not an independent rival to God.

It is part of the created environment described in the opening scene.

This is theologically significant in the ancient context because surrounding cultures sometimes associated cosmic waters with divine beings or primordial deities.

Genesis strips the waters of deity.

There is no battle between God and a water god.

There is no cosmic struggle between equal divine powers.

There is simply:

**God.**

And beneath His sovereign authority:

**the deep.**

---

# 8. “And the Spirit of God” — וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים (*wərûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm*)

One of the most theologically significant phrases in Genesis 1:2 is:

**וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים**

*wərûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm*

Traditionally:

**“And the Spirit of God…”**

The word **רוּחַ (*rûaḥ*)** has a broad semantic range.

It can refer to:

- wind
- breath
- spirit

The precise sense must therefore be determined from context.

The traditional Christian interpretation understands this phrase as referring to the **Spirit of God**, the Holy Spirit.

This reading fits the wider canonical revelation concerning God's Spirit and His activity in creation.

Genesis itself therefore introduces an important distinction:

**God creates.**

And immediately within the creation account:

**the Spirit of God is present and active.**

The complete doctrine of the Trinity is not explicitly articulated in Genesis 1:2.

Nevertheless, the verse provides an important foundation for the later biblical revelation concerning the Father, Son, and Spirit.

---

# 9. “Hovering” — מְרַחֶפֶת (*mərāḥep̄eṯ*)

The verb:

**מְרַחֶפֶת (*mərāḥep̄eṯ*)**

is derived from the root **רחף (*r-ḥ-p*)**.

It conveys the idea of **hovering, moving, or fluttering over** something.

The image is one of active presence.

The Spirit of God is not portrayed as absent from the creation scene.

He is present over the waters.

The wording communicates God's active involvement in creation.

This is important because Genesis 1 is not presenting creation as a mechanical process detached from God.

God is actively present.

God speaks.

God separates.

God names.

God blesses.

God creates.

And here:

**the Spirit of God hovers over the waters.**

---

# 10. “Over the face of the waters” — עַל־פְּנֵי הַמָּיִם (*ʿal-pənê hammāyim*)

The final phrase is:

**עַל־פְּנֵי הַמָּיִם**

*ʿal-pənê hammāyim*

**“over the face of the waters.”**

The noun:

**מַיִם (*mayim*)**

means:

**“waters.”**

It is normally presented in a plural form in Hebrew.

The waters dominate the immediate scene.

The earth is:

**תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ — formless and empty.**

Darkness is:

**over the face of the deep.**

And the Spirit is:

**hovering over the face of the waters.**

The narrative is therefore poised for divine action.

---

# 11. The theological movement of Genesis 1:2

Genesis 1:2 should not be isolated from what follows.

The verse establishes the initial condition:

**Formlessness**

**Emptiness**

**Darkness**

**Deep waters**

But the next verses will introduce God's creative ordering Word.

The progression is therefore:

**Genesis 1:2**

Formless → God gives form.

Empty → God fills.

Dark → God gives light.

Unordered → God establishes distinctions.

Uninhabited → God creates living creatures.

This establishes one of the great themes of Genesis 1:

**God brings order, fullness, distinction, and life out of an initially unformed and unfilled creation.**

---

# 12. Genesis 1:2 and divine sovereignty

There is no indication in the verse that God is struggling to gain control over creation.

The deep does not challenge Him.

The darkness does not oppose Him.

The waters do not threaten Him.

Instead, the Spirit of God is already present over the waters.

Then God speaks.

This establishes a profound biblical contrast:

**Creation does not determine God's authority.**

**God determines creation's order.**

The universe does not organize God.

God organizes the universe.

---

# 13. Genesis 1:2 and the Creator–creature distinction

Genesis 1:2 continues the Creator–creature distinction established in verse 1.

The earth is not God.

The deep is not God.

The waters are not God.

Darkness is not God.

They are all presented as elements within the created order.

The Spirit of God, however, is distinguished from these things.

Thus the verse reinforces the fundamental biblical worldview:

**God is Creator.**

**Everything else is creation.**

This prevents the biblical account from collapsing God into nature.

---

# 14. Genesis 1:2 and the Holy Spirit

Christian theology sees a significant connection between Genesis 1:2 and the broader biblical doctrine of the Holy Spirit.

Genesis presents:

**The Spirit of God present over creation.**

The New Testament later reveals the Spirit's divine identity and continuing work.

This should be handled carefully.

Genesis 1:2 does not provide a complete systematic treatment of the Holy Spirit.

It does, however, introduce **God's Spirit as active at the very beginning of the biblical creation narrative**.

The Christian reader therefore sees an important canonical pattern:

**Father — Creator**

**Son — Creator**

**Spirit — active in creation**

The fullness of this Trinitarian revelation emerges through the entire canon rather than from Genesis 1:2 alone.

---

# 15. Genesis 1:2 and John 1

Genesis 1:2 should also be read alongside the opening of John's Gospel.

Genesis begins:

**“In the beginning God created…”**

John begins:

**“In the beginning was the Word…”**

Genesis presents God's creative activity.

John reveals the Word through whom all things were made.

The relationship is therefore profound:

**Genesis 1 — God creates.**

**John 1 — the Word is present in the beginning and all things are made through Him.**

The New Testament therefore provides further revelation concerning the divine agent of creation.

Genesis gives the foundation.

John gives fuller Christological revelation.

---

# 16. Genesis 1:2 and the doctrine of creation

Genesis 1:2 teaches that creation is not presented as something autonomous.

The earth exists under God's authority.

The waters exist under God's authority.

The deep exists under God's authority.

The Spirit of God is actively present.

And the subsequent creation commands demonstrate that God's Word governs the entire process.

Creation therefore depends upon God.

It does not possess independent existence.

This reinforces the theological distinction established in Genesis 1:1:

**God is eternal and uncreated.**

**Creation is dependent and created.**

---

# 17. Genesis 1:2 and the question of chaos

The word “chaos” must be used carefully when discussing this verse.

Genesis certainly describes an earth that is **unformed and unfilled**, with darkness and deep waters.

But Genesis does not present chaos as an independent deity or eternal principle competing with God.

The biblical worldview is radically different from a dualistic worldview in which good and evil, order and chaos, or two divine powers exist eternally beside one another.

Genesis presents one sovereign God.

The deep is not God's equal.

Darkness is not God's equal.

Creation is not God's equal.

Everything remains under God's sovereign authority.

---

# 18. Literary function within Genesis

Genesis 1:2 functions as a transition between the opening declaration of verse 1 and the divine commands beginning in verse 3.

Verse 1 establishes:

**God created the heavens and the earth.**

Verse 2 describes:

**the initial condition of the earth.**

Verse 3 begins:

**God's ordering command.**

The literary movement is therefore:

**Declaration → Condition → Divine command**

This makes verse 2 essential to understanding the narrative structure.

It establishes the setting into which God's creative Word speaks.

---

# 19. Genesis 1:2 and God's Word

Although the explicit divine speech begins in verse 3, verse 2 prepares the reader for it.

The world is:

**formless**

**empty**

**dark**

**covered by waters**

and then God speaks.

This creates one of the defining rhythms of Genesis 1:

**God speaks → creation responds.**

The following verses repeatedly use the expression:

**“And God said…”**

The creation account therefore emphasizes not merely God's power but God's **authoritative Word**.

---

# 20. The central theological proposition

Genesis 1:2 can be summarized in this theological assertion:

**The earth, initially unformed and unfilled and covered in darkness and waters, exists entirely under the sovereign presence and authority of God, whose Spirit is actively present as He prepares creation for its ordered fullness.**

Or more simply:

**Before God ordered creation, the earth was unformed and empty; but God's Spirit was already present, and God's sovereign Word would bring order, fullness, light, and life.**

---

# Exegetical Outline

## Genesis 1:2

### **“And the earth”**

→ The earth is part of God's created order.

→ Creation is distinct from God.

### **“Was without form and void”**

→ The earth was not yet ordered and filled.

→ God will progressively bring form and fullness.

### **“And darkness”**

→ Darkness is part of the created scene.

→ Darkness is not a divine power competing with God.

### **“Was over the face of the deep”**

→ The deep waters dominate the initial scene.

→ The waters are creatures, not gods.

### **“And the Spirit of God”**

→ God's Spirit is present in creation.

→ The verse provides an important foundation for later Trinitarian revelation.

### **“Was hovering”**

→ God's Spirit is actively present.

→ Creation is under divine activity.

### **“Over the face of the waters”**

→ The entire scene remains under God's sovereign authority.

---

# Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 1:2 establishes or contributes to at least these major doctrines:

1. **Creation's dependence** — creation does not exist independently of God.
2. **Divine sovereignty** — God possesses absolute authority over the created order.
3. **Creator–creature distinction** — the earth, waters, and deep are not divine.
4. **Divine order** — God brings structure to what is initially unformed.
5. **Divine fullness** — God fills what is initially empty.
6. **Divine light** — darkness does not have ultimate authority.
7. **Holy Spirit's activity** — God's Spirit is present and active in creation.
8. **Monotheism** — there is no rival creator or competing deity.
9. **Trinitarian framework** — the Spirit appears in the creation narrative, while fuller Trinitarian revelation comes later.
10. **Divine Word** — the following verses demonstrate God's creative authority through speech.
11. **Providence** — creation remains under God's continuing authority.
12. **Biblical worldview** — creation is ordered according to God's sovereign purpose.

---

# A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

If Genesis 1:2 were being developed into a Bible study or sermon, it could be structured around three propositions:

## I. CREATION WAS INITIALLY UNFORMED

**“The earth was without form and void…”**

Creation had not yet been ordered into its appointed structure.

God would give it form.

---

## II. CREATION WAS INITIALLY DARK

**“And darkness was over the face of the deep…”**

Darkness is present, but it is not sovereign.

It is not God's equal.

The darkness awaits the command of the Creator.

---

## III. GOD'S SPIRIT WAS PRESENT

**“And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters.”**

Before the first recorded command of creation:

**the Spirit of God was already present.**

God is not absent from His creation.

He is actively present and sovereignly preparing the created order for what follows.

---

# Main Proposition

**Genesis 1:2 teaches that the earth, though initially unformed, empty, dark, and covered by the deep waters, remained completely under God's sovereign authority, with God's Spirit actively present as God prepared to bring order and fullness through His Word.**

And the movement from Genesis 1:2 to verse 3 is beautiful:

**Genesis 1:2**

Formlessness.

Emptiness.

Darkness.

Waters.

**Genesis 1:3**

**“And God said, ‘Let there be light.’”**

The God who encounters an unformed and empty creation does not struggle against it.

**He speaks.**

And creation begins to take shape according to His Word.
