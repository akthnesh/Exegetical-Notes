# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:5

**Genesis 1:5 —** “God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night. And there was evening and there was morning, the first day.”

— ESV

---

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים לָאוֹר יוֹם וְלַחֹשֶׁךְ קָרָא לָיְלָה וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר יוֹם אֶחָד**

---

## Transliteration

*Wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm lāʾôr yôm wəlaḥōšeḵ qārāʾ lāylāh wayəhî-ʿerev wayəhî-vōqer yôm ʾeḥād*

---

## Literal rendering

**“And God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. And there was evening, and there was morning, one day.”**

---

# 1. “And God called” — וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים (*wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*)

Genesis 1:5 begins with:

**וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God called…”**

The verb **וַיִּקְרָא (*wayyiqrāʾ*)** comes from the Hebrew root **קרא (*q-r-ʾ*)**, meaning **“to call,” “to proclaim,” “to name,” or “to summon,”** depending upon context.

Here the verb concerns God's act of assigning names.

This is significant because naming in the biblical creation account is not presented as a meaningless labeling exercise.

God is establishing distinctions and assigning designations within the created order.

Genesis 1:4 already stated that God separated the light from the darkness.

Genesis 1:5 now records that God gives names to those two realms:

**Light → Day**

**Darkness → Night**

Thus the verse continues the movement from:

**creation → separation → designation.**

---

# 2. Naming and divine authority

Throughout Genesis, naming can communicate authority, identity, function, or relationship.

Here God names the created realities Himself.

The light does not name itself.

The darkness does not name itself.

Creation does not determine its own ultimate designation.

God names.

This reinforces the Creator–creature distinction.

The One who created the world possesses the authority to define and order it.

The act of naming therefore contributes to the biblical doctrine of divine sovereignty.

God does not merely create reality.

He establishes its order and assigns its designations.

---

# 3. “The light” — לָאוֹר (*lāʾôr*)

The phrase:

**לָאוֹר (*lāʾôr*)**

means:

**“the light.”**

The noun **אוֹר (*ʾôr*)** is the same word introduced in Genesis 1:3 and evaluated as good in Genesis 1:4.

The preposition **לְ (*lə-*)**, here appearing as **לָ (*lā-*)**, connects the act of naming with its object.

Thus:

**לָאוֹר**

**“to/for the light.”**

The narrative then supplies the name:

**יוֹם (*yôm*) — “Day.”**

---

# 4. “Day” — יוֹם (*yôm*)

The Hebrew word:

**יוֹם (*yôm*)**

can refer to:

- day
- daylight
- a period of time
- a particular day

Its precise meaning depends upon context.

In Genesis 1:5, the word is associated directly with the light:

**“God called the light Day.”**

The verse therefore establishes the relationship between the created light and the designation **Day**.

The narrative then distinguishes this from:

**Night.**

Thus the first day is characterized by an ordered cycle involving light and darkness.

---

# 5. “And the darkness he called Night” — וְלַחֹשֶׁךְ קָרָא לָיְלָה

The second naming statement is:

**וְלַחֹשֶׁךְ קָרָא לָיְלָה**

*Wəlaḥōšeḵ qārāʾ lāylāh*

**“And the darkness He called Night.”**

The noun **חֹשֶׁךְ (*ḥōšeḵ*)** is the same word used in Genesis 1:2 and 1:4:

**“darkness.”**

The noun **לַיְלָה (*laylāh*)** means:

**“night.”**

The structure intentionally parallels the first naming:

**Light → Day**

**Darkness → Night**

God establishes an ordered relationship between the two.

---

# 6. Day and Night

Genesis 1:5 therefore establishes the basic distinction between:

**Day**

and

**Night.**

This distinction will become important throughout the creation narrative.

The light and darkness that were distinguished in verse 4 are now given names.

The created order is therefore becoming increasingly structured.

The movement is:

**Genesis 1:2 — formless and empty**

→

**Genesis 1:3 — light created**

→

**Genesis 1:4 — light separated from darkness**

→

**Genesis 1:5 — Day and Night named**

The creation account is progressively moving from an initially unformed condition toward an ordered world.

---

# 7. Naming as an act of ordering

The naming of Day and Night demonstrates that creation has become intelligible and ordered.

God establishes categories within the created world.

Light is not simply present.

It belongs to an ordered cycle called **Day**.

Darkness is not simply present.

It belongs to an ordered cycle called **Night**.

This reveals that God's creative work includes not merely bringing things into existence but establishing their functions and relationships.

---

# 8. “And there was evening” — וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב (*wayəhî-ʿerev*)

The narrative now introduces the first temporal marker:

**וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב**

*Wayəhî-ʿerev*

**“And there was evening.”**

The noun:

**עֶרֶב (*ʿerev*)**

means:

**“evening.”**

The expression connects the created light-darkness cycle with the passage of time.

The narrative is no longer merely describing objects and conditions.

It is beginning to describe an ordered temporal sequence.

---

# 9. “And there was morning” — וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר (*wayəhî-vōqer*)

The corresponding phrase is:

**וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר**

*Wayəhî-vōqer*

**“And there was morning.”**

The noun:

**בֹּקֶר (*bōqer*)**

means:

**“morning.”**

The pairing is:

**evening → morning**

This establishes the temporal framework of the creation days.

The same basic formula will recur throughout Genesis 1.

The creation account therefore presents creation as unfolding according to an ordered sequence of days.

---

# 10. “The first day” — יוֹם אֶחָד (*yôm ʾeḥād*)

The verse concludes:

**יוֹם אֶחָד**

*yôm ʾeḥād*

Literally:

**“one day.”**

The word **אֶחָד (*ʾeḥād*)** means:

**“one.”**

The expression is noteworthy because Genesis does not conclude this first cycle with the ordinal expression “first day” in the Hebrew text.

It literally says:

**“one day.”**

The ESV renders the phrase:

**“the first day.”**

This rendering communicates the narrative function of the expression: this is the first day of the creation sequence.

The Hebrew wording nevertheless deserves attention because **אֶחָד** emphasizes the beginning of the numbered sequence.

---

# 11. The first day

Genesis 1:5 therefore establishes the first complete creation-day cycle.

The sequence is:

**God creates light.**

**God sees that the light is good.**

**God separates light from darkness.**

**God names the light Day.**

**God names the darkness Night.**

**Evening occurs.**

**Morning occurs.**

**One day is completed.**

This is the first explicit temporal framework in the biblical creation account.

---

# 12. God's authority over time

Genesis 1:5 has implications beyond the naming of light and darkness.

Time itself is presented as belonging to God's ordered creation.

Day and night are not autonomous forces.

They are part of the structure established by God.

Later Genesis 1:14–18 will describe the heavenly lights as governing the day and night and serving as signs for seasons, days, and years.

Thus the biblical account presents time as something ordered according to God's purposes.

God is Lord not only over matter but also over the rhythms within creation.

---

# 13. Genesis 1:5 and the goodness of creation

The light had already been declared good in verse 4.

Now the created order receives structure.

Nothing in verse 5 suggests that night is an evil deity or that darkness has escaped God's control.

Night belongs to the order God has established.

This is important because later biblical symbolism sometimes uses darkness metaphorically for evil or judgment.

But Genesis 1:5 itself is not teaching that nighttime is morally evil.

The darkness is part of the created temporal order.

God governs it.

---

# 14. Day and Night are creatures, not gods

Ancient cultures often associated celestial and natural phenomena with divine beings.

Genesis radically demythologizes the world.

Day is not a god.

Night is not a goddess.

Light is not divine.

Darkness is not divine.

The sun, moon, and stars introduced later are not gods either.

They are all subject to the Creator.

Genesis therefore presents a world stripped of competing deities.

There is one sovereign God who gives the created order its structure and meaning.

---

# 15. Genesis 1:5 and monotheism

The verse reinforces the monotheistic framework established in Genesis 1:1.

There is one God who:

**creates**

**separates**

**names**

**orders**

and **governs**.

There is no rival creator.

There is no competing god responsible for darkness.

There is no separate deity governing night.

Everything remains under the authority of the one Creator.

---

# 16. Genesis 1:5 and the authority of naming

God's naming of Day and Night also points toward a broader biblical theme.

The Creator determines the identity and function of His creation.

This is especially important when compared with later passages where God gives humans the responsibility of naming animals.

Human naming occurs under God's prior authority.

God first establishes the ordered world.

Human beings later exercise delegated authority within it.

Thus Genesis 1:5 contributes to the biblical theology of divine authority and delegated human stewardship.

---

# 17. Genesis 1:5 and the structure of the creation week

The expression:

**“And there was evening and there was morning…”**

becomes a recurring formula.

It marks the completion of each creation day.

This gives Genesis 1 a highly structured literary form.

The reader is repeatedly brought through:

**divine command → creative result → divine evaluation → temporal completion.**

The first day therefore establishes the pattern that will characterize the remaining creation narrative.

---

# 18. Genesis 1:5 and the question of the creation days

The expression **יוֹם (*yôm*)** has generated considerable discussion concerning the nature of the creation days.

The word can have different meanings in different contexts.

In Genesis 1:5, it clearly refers to the period associated with the light and distinguished from the night.

The phrase:

**“evening and morning”**

also gives the day a defined temporal structure.

Different Christian traditions have understood the relationship between the creation days and ordinary calendar days differently.

The important exegetical observation from this verse is that Genesis deliberately presents creation through a sequence of distinct, ordered days.

---

# 19. Genesis 1:5 and God's sovereignty over darkness

Genesis 1:2 introduced darkness.

Genesis 1:3 introduced light.

Genesis 1:4 separated light from darkness.

Genesis 1:5 names both.

This progression demonstrates that darkness becomes increasingly subordinated to God's ordering authority.

The darkness is not eliminated.

Instead, God assigns it its proper place within the created order.

This is significant.

God's sovereignty does not necessarily mean that every created distinction disappears.

Rather, God establishes each element according to its appointed function.

---

# 20. Genesis 1:5 and divine wisdom

The naming of Day and Night reveals divine wisdom in ordering creation.

A functioning world requires distinctions.

Day and night establish rhythms that will later govern:

- human activity
- animal activity
- seasons
- agricultural cycles
- worship patterns
- rest

The creation narrative therefore portrays God as establishing an ordered environment suitable for life.

The world is not merely created.

It is prepared.

---

# 21. Genesis 1:5 and God's providence

The ordering of day and night also contributes to the biblical doctrine of providence.

God establishes recurring rhythms within creation.

These rhythms will continue throughout the biblical story.

Day follows night.

Morning follows evening.

The created order operates according to patterns established by God.

Later Scripture repeatedly appeals to the regularity of these created cycles as evidence of God's faithfulness and sovereign rule.

---

# 22. Genesis 1:5 and John 1

The light introduced in Genesis becomes especially significant when read alongside John's Gospel.

Genesis says:

**“God called the light Day.”**

John declares concerning the Word:

**“In him was life, and the life was the light of men.”**

John then identifies Christ as the **true light**.

The physical light of Genesis and the spiritual imagery of John's Gospel should not be confused.

Genesis 1:5 is describing the created order.

John develops the biblical theme of light toward Christological fulfillment.

The canonical relationship is therefore:

**Created light → biblical light → Christ the Light.**

---

# 23. Genesis 1:5 and Christ as the Light

The New Testament identifies Jesus as:

**“the light of the world.”**

This language takes on deeper significance when viewed against the creation background.

The One through whom all things were made is also revealed as the One who brings spiritual light to humanity.

Genesis therefore provides foundational imagery that later Scripture uses to communicate the glory of Christ.

The connection is canonical rather than a claim that Genesis 1:5 directly names Christ.

---

# 24. Genesis 1:5 and new creation

The light-darkness distinction also points forward within the broader biblical narrative.

The Bible begins with God establishing light in creation.

It ultimately reaches the new creation, where the presence and glory of God provide illumination.

The biblical story therefore moves from:

**creation**

to

**fall**

to

**redemption**

to

**new creation.**

The God who established Day and Night in the beginning remains sovereign over history until the consummation of all things.

---

# 25. Literary function within Genesis

Genesis 1:5 concludes the first major creation sequence.

It accomplishes several literary purposes:

1. It names the light.
2. It names the darkness.
3. It establishes Day and Night.
4. It introduces evening and morning.
5. It identifies the first creation day.

This creates the template for the subsequent days.

The verse therefore functions as both a conclusion to the first creative act and a structural pattern for the rest of Genesis 1.

---

# 26. The central theological proposition

If Genesis 1:5 is reduced to its fundamental theological assertion, it could be stated this way:

**God sovereignly establishes the temporal order of creation by naming the light Day and the darkness Night, demonstrating His authority over the rhythms, boundaries, and functions of the created world.**

Or more simply:

**God creates, separates, names, and orders the rhythms of creation according to His sovereign wisdom.**

---

# Exegetical Outline

## Genesis 1:5

### **“God called the light Day”**

→ God has authority to name His creation.

→ Light is assigned its proper function and designation.

### **“And the darkness he called Night”**

→ Darkness remains under God's authority.

→ Night is part of the ordered created world.

### **“And there was evening”**

→ Creation is placed within an ordered temporal sequence.

### **“And there was morning”**

→ The temporal cycle progresses according to God's established order.

### **“The first day”**

→ The first creation-day cycle is completed.

→ Genesis establishes the recurring structure for the remaining creation account.

---

# Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 1:5 establishes or contributes to at least these major doctrines:

1. **Divine sovereignty** — God has authority over creation and its order.
2. **Divine authority in naming** — God determines the designations of His creation.
3. **Creator–creature distinction** — Day and Night are created realities, not divine beings.
4. **Divine order** — God establishes distinctions and rhythms within creation.
5. **God's sovereignty over time** — temporal cycles operate according to God's ordering.
6. **Monotheism** — there are no rival gods governing light or darkness.
7. **Goodness of creation** — the day-night cycle belongs to God's ordered creation.
8. **Divine wisdom** — creation is structured according to purposeful design.
9. **Providence** — the recurring rhythms of creation remain under God's sovereign ordering.
10. **Human stewardship** — God's ordering of creation establishes the environment in which humanity will later exercise delegated authority.
11. **Biblical light motif** — light becomes a recurring image throughout Scripture.
12. **Christological fulfillment** — the New Testament develops the light motif in its presentation of Christ as the Light.
13. **New creation** — the creation narrative's light theme ultimately participates in the Bible's movement toward consummated creation.

---

# A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

If Genesis 1:5 were developed into a Bible study or sermon, it could be structured around three propositions:

## I. GOD NAMES HIS CREATION

**“God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night.”**

The Creator establishes the identity and function of His creation.

God has authority over what He has made.

---

## II. GOD ORDERS TIME

**“And there was evening and there was morning…”**

The passage of time is not outside God's sovereignty.

The rhythms of creation operate according to the order established by the Creator.

---

## III. GOD ESTABLISHES THE FIRST DAY

**“…the first day.”**

The first creation cycle establishes the pattern that will continue throughout the creation week.

God is progressively transforming the unformed world into an ordered, habitable creation.

---

# Main Proposition

**Genesis 1:5 teaches that the sovereign Creator establishes the order and rhythm of time by naming the light Day and the darkness Night, demonstrating that even the cycles of light, darkness, evening, and morning remain under His wise and absolute authority.**

The movement from Genesis 1:2 through Genesis 1:5 is therefore remarkable:

**Genesis 1:2**

Darkness over the deep.

**Genesis 1:3**

God speaks:

**“Let there be light.”**

**Genesis 1:4**

God sees that the light is good.

God separates light from darkness.

**Genesis 1:5**

God names them:

**Day and Night.**

Then:

**evening → morning → one day.**

The Creator does not merely create light.

**He orders it.**

He does not merely separate light from darkness.

**He names them.**

And He does not merely create the world.

**He establishes its rhythms according to His sovereign wisdom.**
