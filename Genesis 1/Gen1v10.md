# Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:10

**Genesis 1:10 —** “God called the dry land Earth, and the waters that were gathered together he called Seas. And God saw that it was good.”

— ESV

---

## Hebrew Text

**וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים לַיַּבָּשָׁה אֶרֶץ וּלְמִקְוֵה הַמַּיִם קָרָא יַמִּים וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים כִּי־טוֹב**

---

## Transliteration

*Wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm layyabbāšāh ʾereṣ ûləmiqweh ham-mayim qārāʾ yammîm wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm kî-ṭôb*

---

## Literal rendering

**“And God called the dry land Earth, and the gathering of the waters he called Seas. And God saw that it was good.”**

---

# 1. “And God called” — וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים (*wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*)

Genesis 1:10 begins with the same divine naming formula seen earlier in the chapter:

**וַיִּקְרָא אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyiqrāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God called…”**

God has already named:

- the light **Day**
- the darkness **Night**
- the expanse **Heaven**

Now God names the two major domains established in Genesis 1:9:

- the dry land
- the gathered waters

Naming is part of God's ordering activity.

---

# 2. “The dry land” — לַיַּבָּשָׁה (*layyabbāšāh*)

The phrase:

**לַיַּבָּשָׁה**

*layyabbāšāh*

means:

**“the dry land.”**

The noun **יַבָּשָׁה (*yabbāšāh*)** refers to dry ground or dry land.

This is the land that appeared in verse 9 when God commanded the waters to be gathered.

Verse 10 now gives that land a name.

The sequence is:

**Genesis 1:9 — dry land appears.**

**Genesis 1:10 — God names the dry land Earth.**

---

# 3. “Earth” — אֶרֶץ (*ʾereṣ*)

God calls the dry land:

**אֶרֶץ**

*ʾereṣ*

**“Earth.”**

This is the same Hebrew noun used in Genesis 1:1:

**“the heavens and the earth.”**

The term **אֶרֶץ (*ʾereṣ*)** can refer to:

- earth
- land
- ground
- territory
- country

Its precise meaning depends upon context.

Here it designates the dry-land domain in contrast with the gathered waters.

Thus Genesis 1:10 connects the dry land with the **earth** introduced in Genesis 1:1.

---

# 4. The relationship between Genesis 1:1 and Genesis 1:10

Genesis 1:1 introduced:

**“the heavens and the earth.”**

Genesis 1:10 now identifies the dry-land domain as:

**Earth — אֶרֶץ (*ʾereṣ*)**

This shows how the narrative progressively develops the opening statement.

Genesis 1:1 gives the comprehensive declaration.

Genesis 1:2–10 describes the ordering and preparation of that creation.

The earth is progressively transformed from an unformed and empty condition into an ordered environment.

---

# 5. “And the waters” — וּלְמִקְוֵה הַמַּיִם (*ûləmiqweh ham-māyim*)

The second domain is described as:

**מִקְוֵה הַמַּיִם**

*miqweh ham-māyim*

Literally:

**“the gathering of the waters.”**

The word **מִקְוֶה (*miqweh*)** refers to a gathering or collection.

It is related to the verb used in Genesis 1:9 for the gathering of the waters.

The waters have therefore been given a defined collective domain.

---

# 6. “He called Seas” — קָרָא יַמִּים (*qārāʾ yammîm*)

God calls the gathering of the waters:

**יַמִּים**

*Yammîm*

**“Seas.”**

The singular **יָם (*yām*)** means:

**“sea.”**

The plural **יַמִּים (*yammîm*)** means:

**“seas.”**

God therefore establishes the terminology by which the gathered waters are identified.

The land is:

**Earth.**

The gathered waters are:

**Seas.**

---

# 7. Earth and Seas

Genesis 1:10 therefore establishes a fundamental geographical distinction:

**Earth ↔ Seas**

This continues the pattern of ordered separation throughout Genesis 1.

Earlier:

**Light ↔ Darkness**

**Waters above ↔ Waters below**

Now:

**Earth ↔ Seas**

God establishes a world characterized by distinct domains.

---

# 8. Naming demonstrates divine authority

God's naming of Earth and Seas is significant.

The Creator determines the designation of His creation.

The land does not name itself.

The sea does not name itself.

God names them.

This demonstrates His sovereign authority over the created order.

---

# 9. Earth is not divine

Genesis deliberately presents the earth as something God created and named.

The earth is therefore not God.

It is not an independent divine being.

It is not eternal.

It is not self-existent.

The earth belongs to the category of creation.

God belongs to the category of Creator.

This Creator–creature distinction is foundational to biblical theology.

---

# 10. The Seas are not divine

The same principle applies to the seas.

The seas are created realities.

They are not rival gods.

They are not independent cosmic powers.

God gathers them.

God assigns their domain.

God names them.

The sea therefore exists under the sovereign authority of its Creator.

---

# 11. Genesis 1:10 and ancient Near Eastern religion

The theological significance becomes particularly clear in the ancient context.

Many ancient cultures associated the sea with divine powers or cosmic chaos.

Genesis presents a radically different picture.

God does not have to defeat the sea.

He does not fight a sea deity.

He simply commands the waters to gather.

Then He names them.

The waters obey.

This presents God's sovereignty as absolute.

---

# 12. God establishes the boundaries of the sea

Genesis 1:10 establishes the sea as a defined domain.

Later Scripture repeatedly celebrates God's authority over the sea.

Psalm 95:5 says:

**“The sea is his, for he made it.”**

The theological logic is the same:

**God made it → therefore it belongs to Him.**

Genesis 1:10 provides the foundational creation framework for this later confession.

---

# 13. “And God saw” — וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים (*wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm*)

The final section begins:

**וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים**

*Wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm*

**“And God saw…”**

This is the first explicit appearance of the phrase:

**“God saw…”**

in the third day's account.

The creation narrative repeatedly presents God evaluating His work.

God does not merely create.

He also evaluates creation according to His own standard.

---

# 14. “That it was good” — כִּי־טוֹב (*kî-ṭôb*)

The verse concludes:

**כִּי־טוֹב**

*kî-ṭôb*

**“that it was good.”**

The adjective:

**טוֹב (*ṭôb*)**

means:

**“good.”**

This is the recurring goodness declaration of Genesis 1.

God Himself evaluates the created order and declares it good.

---

# 15. The goodness of physical creation

This statement is theologically significant.

The physical world is described as **good**.

The land is good.

The seas are good.

The material creation is not inherently evil.

Genesis therefore contradicts any worldview in which physical matter is inherently opposed to spiritual goodness.

Creation comes from God and receives His positive evaluation.

---

# 16. “Good” means more than merely pleasant

The Hebrew **טוֹב (*ṭôb*)** can communicate goodness, suitability, beauty, benefit, or fitness depending on context.

Here, the declaration indicates that God's creation conforms to His purposes.

The land and seas are properly ordered.

They occupy their intended domains.

They are suitable within the larger creative design.

The goodness of creation is therefore connected with God's own evaluation and purpose.

---

# 17. God's evaluation is authoritative

The text does not say that humanity looked at the earth and decided it was good.

God saw it.

God declared it good.

The ultimate standard for the goodness of creation is therefore God's own judgment.

This establishes an important biblical principle:

**God determines what is good.**

Creation receives its evaluation from its Creator.

---

# 18. Genesis 1:10 and divine wisdom

The earth and seas are now properly distinguished.

This is not merely a rearrangement of physical matter.

It is the establishment of an environment capable of supporting what follows.

The next verse will introduce vegetation.

The land must therefore exist as a distinct domain before vegetation can be commanded to grow upon it.

The sequence reveals purposeful design.

---

# 19. Genesis 1:10 prepares for vegetation

Verse 10 establishes:

**Earth**

and

**Seas.**

Verse 11 will immediately command the earth to produce vegetation.

The literary connection is intentional.

God establishes the environment.

Then He fills it.

This is a major structural feature of Genesis 1.

---

# 20. The forming and filling pattern

Genesis 1 can be understood as moving through two broad phases.

### Forming

**Day 1**

Light and darkness.

**Day 2**

Heavens and waters.

**Day 3**

Earth and seas.

### Filling

**Day 4**

Heavenly lights.

**Day 5**

Sea creatures and birds.

**Day 6**

Land animals and humanity.

Genesis 1:10 belongs to the forming stage.

The earth is being prepared for the life that will later inhabit it.

---

# 21. Earth and Seas as ordered domains

The verse presents the world as an ordered arrangement:

**Earth**

and

**Seas.**

The land is not everywhere.

The waters are not everywhere.

Each has an assigned domain.

This illustrates one of the chapter's most important themes:

**God creates order through boundaries.**

---

# 22. Genesis 1:10 and divine providence

The order established here becomes the foundation for the continuing existence of the created world.

The distinction between land and sea is part of the stable order God established.

The later biblical writers frequently appeal to creation as evidence of God's continuing sovereignty.

The God who established the boundaries of the seas remains sovereign over them.

---

# 23. Genesis 1:10 and God's ownership

The naming of Earth and Seas also establishes divine ownership.

Psalm 24:1 declares:

**“The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof.”**

The reason is rooted in creation:

**“for he has founded it upon the seas and established it upon the rivers.”**

Genesis 1 provides the foundation for this theology.

The earth belongs to God because God made it.

---

# 24. Genesis 1:10 and humanity's future stewardship

Humanity has not yet appeared.

But the world in which humanity will live is already being established.

When humanity later receives dominion over the earth, that dominion will be delegated.

Humans will never become the ultimate owners of creation.

God remains the Creator and rightful Lord of the earth.

---

# 25. The goodness of land and sea

Both domains receive God's approval.

This is significant.

God does not say only that the dry land is good.

The gathered waters and dry land together form part of the good created order.

The biblical worldview therefore recognizes the goodness of the physical environment.

---

# 26. Genesis 1:10 and environmental responsibility

The goodness of creation provides an important theological foundation for responsible stewardship.

Because creation belongs to God and is declared good by God, it should not be treated as meaningless or disposable.

Human beings are later commanded to exercise dominion.

Biblical dominion is therefore best understood as accountable stewardship under God's authority rather than unrestricted exploitation.

---

# 27. Genesis 1:10 and the sea throughout Scripture

The sea becomes a recurring biblical symbol and setting.

It appears in:

- creation
- the Flood
- the Exodus
- Israel's poetry
- prophetic literature
- the ministry of Jesus
- the book of Revelation

Yet Genesis establishes its most fundamental identity:

**the sea belongs to the Creator.**

Even when Scripture uses the sea symbolically, its ultimate sovereignty remains God's.

---

# 28. Genesis 1:10 and God's authority over chaos

The sea can later become associated poetically with danger, instability, and chaos.

But Genesis establishes that the sea itself is subject to God.

The Creator establishes its boundaries.

Thus even the imagery of chaotic waters never implies that God lacks ultimate control.

---

# 29. Genesis 1:10 and the biblical doctrine of creation

This verse contributes to several foundational truths about creation:

**Creation is not self-existent.**

**Creation is not divine.**

**Creation is ordered.**

**Creation belongs to God.**

**Creation is evaluated by God.**

**Creation is good.**

These themes will shape biblical theology throughout Scripture.

---

# 30. Genesis 1:10 and God's transcendence

God is distinct from the earth and seas He creates.

He stands above the created order as its sovereign Creator.

The earth does not contain Him.

The sea does not control Him.

The heavens do not limit Him.

Everything exists beneath His authority.

---

# 31. Genesis 1:10 and God's immanence

Yet God is actively involved in creation.

He gathers the waters.

He establishes the land.

He names both.

He evaluates them.

God is therefore not detached from the physical world.

He actively orders and governs it.

---

# 32. Genesis 1:10 and monotheism

There is one God naming both domains.

The earth does not belong to one god while the sea belongs to another.

Both belong to the same Creator.

This is a fundamental biblical rejection of polytheistic cosmology.

The entire created order has one sovereign Lord.

---

# 33. Genesis 1:10 and Christological creation

Genesis 1:10 does not explicitly mention Christ.

However, the New Testament teaches that all things were created through Christ.

John 1:3 declares:

**“All things were made through him.”**

Colossians 1:16 states:

**“All things were created through him and for him.”**

Therefore, within the complete biblical canon, the earth and seas described in Genesis 1:10 belong within the Son's creative work as well.

---

# 34. Genesis 1:10 and Christ's lordship

Because Christ is presented in the New Testament as Creator, His lordship extends over the physical creation.

The Gospels even portray Jesus exercising authority over the sea.

He rebukes the wind and sea, and they obey Him.

This is consistent with the Genesis picture of divine authority over the waters.

---

# 35. Genesis 1:10 and the new creation

The biblical story begins with the ordering of earth and sea.

It ultimately moves toward the consummation of God's purposes in the new creation.

Revelation 21 describes:

**“a new heaven and a new earth.”**

The Bible therefore begins and ends with God's sovereignty over creation.

Genesis 1:10 establishes the original order.

The final biblical vision presents creation brought to its consummated state.

---

# 36. Literary significance of the verse

Genesis 1:10 performs three major literary functions:

### 1. Naming

**Earth**

**Seas**

### 2. Ordering

The land and waters occupy distinct domains.

### 3. Evaluation

**“God saw that it was good.”**

Thus the verse moves from:

**identity → order → goodness.**

---

# 37. The relationship between verses 9 and 10

Genesis 1:9:

**God commands the waters to gather.**

**Dry land appears.**

Genesis 1:10:

**God names the dry land Earth.**

**God names the gathered waters Seas.**

**God declares the arrangement good.**

The two verses form a tightly connected sequence:

**Command → accomplishment → naming → evaluation.**

This is characteristic of Genesis 1.

---

# 38. The progression of divine ordering

The creation narrative has now established several major distinctions:

**Day ↔ Night**

**Heaven ↔ Earth**

**Waters ↔ Dry Land**

The world is no longer portrayed as an undifferentiated watery environment.

It has recognizable domains.

God is transforming creation into an ordered dwelling place.

---

# 39. The central theological proposition

If Genesis 1:10 is reduced to its fundamental theological assertion, it could be stated this way:

**God sovereignly names the dry land Earth and the gathered waters Seas, then declares their ordered arrangement good, demonstrating His authority over creation and the goodness of the physical world He has made.**

Or more simply:

**God establishes, names, and declares good the ordered earth and seas.**

---

# Exegetical Outline

## Genesis 1:10

### **“God called the dry land Earth”**

→ God gives the dry land its designation.

→ The earth belongs to the created order.

### **“And the waters that were gathered together he called Seas”**

→ God identifies the gathered waters as Seas.

→ The seas remain under divine authority.

### **“And God saw”**

→ God evaluates His creative work.

→ His judgment establishes the standard of goodness.

### **“That it was good”**

→ The ordered physical creation is good.

→ The land and seas fulfill their intended role within God's design.

---

# Doctrinal Implications

Genesis 1:10 establishes or contributes to at least these major doctrines:

1. **Divine sovereignty** — God has authority over earth and seas.
2. **Divine naming** — God determines the designation of His creation.
3. **Creator–creature distinction** — earth and seas are created realities, not gods.
4. **Monotheism** — one God rules the entire created order.
5. **Divine order** — God establishes distinct domains within creation.
6. **Divine boundaries** — God determines the domain of the waters and land.
7. **Goodness of creation** — God explicitly declares the ordered creation good.
8. **Goodness of the physical world** — material creation is not inherently evil.
9. **Divine wisdom** — the world is ordered according to purposeful design.
10. **Divine ownership** — the earth and seas ultimately belong to their Creator.
11. **Human stewardship** — humanity's later dominion takes place within God's creation and under His authority.
12. **God's sovereignty over the sea** — the waters are subject to His command.
13. **Rejection of cosmic dualism** — the sea is not a rival power to God.
14. **Rejection of nature worship** — the earth and seas are creation, not Creator.
15. **Divine providence** — the ordered world remains dependent upon God's governance.
16. **Christological creation** — the New Testament identifies Christ as the One through whom all things were created.
17. **Christ's lordship over creation** — Christ's authority extends over the created order.
18. **New creation** — the ordered earth of Genesis anticipates the biblical hope of renewed creation.

# A Concise Preaching/Exposition Framework

If Genesis 1:10 were developed into a Bible study or sermon, it could be structured around three propositions:

## I. GOD DEFINES THE CREATED ORDER

**“God called the dry land Earth…”**

God establishes the identity and boundaries of the physical world.

---

## II. GOD OWNS THE SEAS

**“The waters that were gathered together he called Seas.”**

The seas are not divine rivals.

They belong to the Creator and remain under His authority.

---

## III. GOD DECLARES HIS CREATION GOOD

**“And God saw that it was good.”**

The physical world is God's good creation.

Its goodness rests ultimately upon the Creator's own evaluation and purpose.

---

# Main Proposition

**Genesis 1:10 teaches that God sovereignly names the dry land Earth and the gathered waters Seas, then declares their ordered arrangement good, demonstrating that the physical world belongs to Him, exists according to His wise design, and possesses goodness because it comes from the hand of its Creator.**

The movement from Genesis 1:2 through Genesis 1:10 is therefore remarkable:

**Genesis 1:2**

Darkness.

Waters.

An unformed and empty world.

**Genesis 1:3**

God speaks:

**“Let there be light.”**

**Genesis 1:4**

God separates:

**light from darkness.**

**Genesis 1:5**

God names:

**Day and Night.**

**Genesis 1:6**

God speaks:

**“Let there be an expanse…”**

**Genesis 1:7**

God establishes:

**the waters above and below the expanse.**

**Genesis 1:8**

God names the expanse:

**Heaven.**

**Genesis 1:9**

God speaks:

**“Let the waters … be gathered together…”**

And:

**“let the dry land appear.”**

**Genesis 1:10**

God names:

**Dry Land → Earth**

**Gathered Waters → Seas**

Then God evaluates His work:

**“And God saw that it was good.”**

The pattern continues:

**God speaks.**

**God acts.**

**God separates.**

**God gathers.**

**God names.**

**God evaluates.**

**Creation obeys.**

**Creation is good.**
