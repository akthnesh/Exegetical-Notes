**Exegetical Notes on Genesis 1:1**

**Genesis 1:1 —** “In the beginning God created the
heavens and the earth.”

— ESV

**Hebrew Text**

**בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ**

**Transliteration:**

*Berēʾšît bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾēt haššāmayim wəʾēt hāʾāreṣ*

**Literal rendering:**

**“In beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.”**

---

**1. “In the beginning” — בְּרֵאשִׁית
(*****berēʾšît*****)**

The verse begins with **בְּרֵאשִׁית
(*****berēʾšît*****)**, commonly translated **“In the beginning.”**

The noun *rēʾšît* means **beginning, first, beginning-point, or first part**.
The prefixed **בְּ (*****be-*****)** generally means “in,”
giving the expression “in the beginning.”

The important theological point is that Genesis does not
begin with an explanation of where God came from.

It begins with **God already existing**.

Genesis does not say:

“In the beginning God came into existence.”

Rather:

**“In the beginning God created…”**

God is therefore presented as existing **before creation**.

This immediately distinguishes the biblical worldview from
pagan creation accounts in which gods themselves arise from some primordial
substance or participate in cosmic conflict.

The biblical text presents God as **prior to and
independent of the created order**.

**Exegetical observation**

The “beginning” here is best understood as the beginning of **the
created universe**, not the beginning of God's existence.

God has no origin.

Creation does.

This becomes foundational to biblical theology:

**God is eternal; creation is contingent.**

Compare:

- Psalm      90:2 — God is “from everlasting to everlasting.” 
- John      1:1–3 — the Word exists “in the beginning” and all things are made through      Him. 
- Hebrews      11:3 — the visible universe was made by God's command. 

---

**2. “God” — אֱלֹהִים (*****ʾĕlōhîm*****)**

The subject of the sentence is:

**אֱלֹהִים (*****ʾĕlōhîm*****) — “God.”**

Interestingly, *ʾĕlōhîm* is grammatically plural in form, yet when referring to
the God of Israel it normally takes **singular verbs and adjectives**.

That is exactly what happens here.

The verb **בָּרָא (*****bārāʾ*****)**, “created,” is **third-person
masculine singular**.

Thus:

**אֱלֹהִים בָּרָא**

*ʾĕlōhîm bārāʾ*

**“God created.”**

The grammar presents **one God acting as the Creator**.

It would therefore be going too far to use the plural form *ʾĕlōhîm*
by itself as proof of the doctrine of the Trinity.

However, Christians can legitimately read Genesis 1:1 **canonically**
in light of later revelation concerning the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Genesis itself progressively introduces God's Spirit (Gen.
1:2), while the New Testament identifies Christ as active in creation (John
1:1–3; Col. 1:16).

So:

**Genesis 1:1 does not fully reveal the Trinity, but it is
fully compatible with—and ultimately illuminated by—the doctrine of the
Trinity.**

---

**3. “Created” — בָּרָא
(*****bārāʾ*****)**

The verb **בָּרָא (*****bārāʾ*****)** means **“to create.”**

This verb is particularly significant because its subject in
the Old Testament is frequently **God**.

Genesis repeatedly uses *bārāʾ* in connection with God's
creative activity:

- Genesis      1:1 — God created the heavens and earth. 
- Genesis      1:21 — God created the great sea creatures. 
- Genesis      1:27 — God created mankind. 
- Genesis      5:1–2 — God created humanity. 
- Isaiah      40:28 — God is the Creator. 

The emphasis is therefore not merely on **manufacturing
something**, but on **God's sovereign creative activity**.

God is the Creator.

Everything else is creature.

That distinction is one of the most fundamental distinctions
in Scripture.

**Creator–creature distinction**

Christian theology must preserve this distinction:

**God ≠ creation.**

God is not merely the greatest thing within the universe.

He is the One **upon whom the entire universe depends for
its existence**.

This stands against pantheism—the belief that God is
identical with everything—and panentheism—the idea that everything exists
within God.

Genesis presents God as **distinct from His creation**.

---

**4. “The heavens” — הַשָּׁמַיִם
(*****haššāmayim*****)**

The Hebrew expression is:

**הַשָּׁמַיִם —** ***haššāmayim***

Literally, **“the heavens.”**

The term can refer to the sky, the atmospheric heavens, or
the celestial realm.

Within Genesis 1, however, the phrase participates in a
comprehensive description of the created universe.

The text does not merely say:

“God created the earth.”

It says:

**“God created the heavens and the earth.”**

This is a Hebrew expression known as a **merism**—two
extremes used to communicate a totality.

For example:

“day and night”

can communicate the entire cycle of time.

Likewise:

**“heavens and earth”**

communicates the **totality of the created order**.

Thus Genesis 1:1 functions as a cosmic declaration:

**Everything that exists apart from God owes its existence
to God.**

---

**5. “And the earth” — וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ
(*****wəʾēt hāʾāreṣ*****)**

The Hebrew word **אֶרֶץ (*****ʾereṣ*****)** can mean **earth,
land, territory, or ground**, depending on context.

Here, in the expression:

**“the heavens and the earth”**

it is best understood in the broad sense of **the earth as
part of the created universe**.

The following verses will explain the condition and ordering
of the earth:

“The earth was without form and void…”

Genesis 1:1 therefore establishes the great theological
premise before the narrative proceeds to describe God's ordering of creation.

---

**6. The word אֵת (*****ʾēt*****)**

You may notice something interesting in the Hebrew:

**אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ**

There are actually two occurrences of **אֵת
(*****ʾēt*****)**.

This word generally functions as the **definite
direct-object marker** in Hebrew.

It normally isn't translated into English.

So:

**בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם**

literally has the grammatical structure:

“God created [direct object marker] the heavens.”

And:

**וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ**

“and [direct object marker] the earth.”

This grammatical construction makes the structure of the
verse particularly clear:

**Subject:** God

**Verb:** created

**Objects:** the heavens and the earth

The emphasis is unmistakable:

**God is the Creator; the heavens and earth are His
creation.**

---

**7. Theological significance: Creation is not God**

Genesis 1:1 destroys the ancient idea that nature itself is
divine.

The sun is not God.

The moon is not God.

The stars are not gods.

The sea is not God.

The earth is not God.

Creation exists because **God created it**.

Interestingly, Genesis will later describe the sun and moon
without even using their ordinary names in the creation account, referring to
them as the **“greater light”** and **“lesser light”** (Gen. 1:16).

This fits the biblical polemic against ancient Near Eastern
cosmologies.

What surrounding cultures could worship as gods, Genesis
presents as **mere creatures** under the sovereign authority of God.

---

**8. Creation establishes God's absolute sovereignty**

Genesis 1:1 establishes a fundamental biblical doctrine:

**God possesses absolute authority over everything because
everything belongs to Him as Creator.**

This theme appears throughout Scripture.

Psalm 24:1 says:

“The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof.”

Why?

Because God created it.

Creation establishes **divine ownership**.

And divine ownership establishes **divine authority**.

God doesn't need permission from creation to rule creation.

He is the Creator.

---

**9. Creation and God's independence**

Genesis 1:1 also establishes God's **aseity**.

*Aseity* means that God exists **from Himself**,
rather than depending upon something outside Himself for His existence.

Creation, however, is completely dependent upon God.

This creates a fundamental distinction:

**10. Genesis 1:1 and creation ex nihilo**

A major theological question is whether Genesis 1:1 teaches **creation
*****ex nihilo***—creation “out of nothing.”

Christian theology historically understands Genesis 1:1
together with the rest of Scripture as teaching that God is the ultimate source
of everything that exists.

Hebrews 11:3 says:

“what is seen was not made out of things that are visible.”

John 1:3 says:

“All things were made through him.”

Romans 4:17 speaks of God as the One who:

“gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things
that do not exist.”

Thus, biblically considered, **nothing exists independently
of God's creative activity**.

There has been considerable discussion among Hebrew scholars
concerning the precise grammatical relationship between Genesis 1:1 and 1:2,
particularly whether verse 1 is an independent sentence or a temporal clause.
But either way, the larger canonical witness clearly teaches God as the
ultimate Creator of all things.

---

**11. Genesis 1:1 and John 1:1**

One of the most beautiful canonical connections is between
Genesis and John's Gospel.

Genesis:

**“In the beginning God created…”**

John:

**“In the beginning was the Word…”**

John deliberately echoes Genesis.

But John takes us further.

The Word was:

- already      existing in the beginning, 
- with      God, 
- and      Himself divine. 

Then John says:

“All things were made through him.”

Thus the New Testament reveals that **the Son was active in
the creation described in Genesis**.

Genesis begins:

**In the beginning, God created.**

John begins:

**In the beginning, the Word was.**

The contrast is profound.

**Creation began.**
**
The Word already was.**

---

**12. Genesis 1:1 and Christ**

The New Testament explicitly attributes creation to Christ.

Colossians 1:16 says that:

“by him all things were created”

and Hebrews 1:2 describes the Son as the One through whom
God made the world.

Therefore, Christians should not read Genesis 1 merely as an
isolated Old Testament account.

The whole biblical revelation eventually gives us:

**Father — Creator**

**Son — Creator**

**Spirit — active in creation**

This does not mean Genesis 1:1 by itself gives us the
complete doctrine of the Trinity.

Rather, **the doctrine of the Trinity is progressively
revealed throughout Scripture**, and Genesis provides the foundational
monotheistic framework into which the later revelation fits.

---

**13. Genesis 1:1 and the doctrine of providence**

Creation isn't merely about how the universe **started**.

It also establishes God's continuing relationship to
creation.

The God who creates also sustains.

Colossians 1:17 says of Christ:

“in him all things hold together.”

Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son as:

“upholding the universe by the word of his power.”

Thus creation is not an event after which God simply steps
away.

The universe remains dependent upon Him.

---

**14. Literary function within Genesis**

Genesis 1:1 serves as the **opening theological declaration**
of the creation account.

The subsequent verses explain how God brings order to the
created world.

There is a movement from:

**formlessness → ordered creation**

and

**emptiness → fullness**

God brings structure, distinction, purpose, and life.

The creation account repeatedly emphasizes God's Word:

**“And God said…”**

God speaks, and creation responds.

This demonstrates that God's sovereignty is exercised
through His authoritative Word.

---

**15. The central theological proposition**

If we reduce Genesis 1:1 to its fundamental theological
assertion, we could state it this way:

**The eternal God is the sovereign Creator of the entire
universe, and everything that exists apart from Him is dependent upon His
creative authority.**

Or even more simply:

**God existed before everything, God created everything,
and therefore everything belongs to God.**

---

**Exegetical Outline**

**Genesis 1:1**

**“In the beginning”**

→ Creation has a beginning.

→ God does not.

→ God is eternal.

**“God”**

→ God is the subject of creation.

→ He is distinct from creation.

→ He is sovereign over creation.

**“created”**

→ Creation originates in God's sovereign action.

→ The universe is not self-existent.

→ Nothing in creation possesses independent existence from God.

**“the heavens”**

→ The celestial realm belongs to God.

**“and the earth”**

→ The terrestrial realm belongs to God.

**Central idea:**

**Before there was a universe, there was God; and the
universe exists because God sovereignly brought it into being.**

---

**Doctrinal Implications**

Genesis 1:1 establishes at least these major doctrines:

1. **God's      eternality** — God precedes creation. 
2. **God's      aseity** — God does not depend upon creation. 
3. **Creation**      — the universe is God's work. 
4. **Divine      sovereignty** — God has absolute authority over creation. 
5. **Creator–creature      distinction** — God is not part of creation. 
6. **Human      accountability** — humanity exists by God's creative will. 
7. **Anti-idolatry**      — creation must not be worshiped as God. 
8. **Providence**      — creation remains dependent upon God. 
9. **Monotheism**      — there is one sovereign Creator. 
10. **Christological      fulfillment** — the New Testament identifies the Son as active in      creation. 
11. **Trinitarian      framework** — Father, Son, and Spirit are revealed in the complete      biblical doctrine of creation. 
12. **Biblical      worldview** — reality begins with God, not matter, chance, or humanity. 

---

**A concise preaching/exposition framework**

If you're preparing this for a **Bible study or sermon**,
I would structure Genesis 1:1 around three propositions:

**I. GOD IS ETERNAL**

**“In the beginning God…”**

Before creation existed, **God existed**.

**II. GOD IS THE CREATOR**

**“…created…”**

The universe is not self-generated. It exists because **God
willed it into existence**.

**III. GOD OWNS ALL CREATION**

**“…the heavens and the earth.”**

Everything—from the highest heavens to the earth beneath our
feet—is ultimately **God's possession and therefore subject to His authority**.

**Main proposition**

**Genesis 1:1 teaches that the eternal God is the sovereign
Creator of all things, establishing His absolute authority over the universe
and everything within it.**

And there is a wonderful gospel trajectory here:

**Genesis 1:1:** God creates the heavens and the earth.

**John 1:1:** The Word is in the beginning.

**John 1:3:** All things are created through Him.

**Colossians 1:16:** All things are created through Christ and for Christ.

**Revelation 21:1:** God brings about a **new heaven and a new earth**.

So Scripture begins with:

**“In the beginning God created…”**

and ultimately moves toward:

**“Behold, I am making all things new.”**

The God who began creation is also the God who will **consummate
and renew creation**.
